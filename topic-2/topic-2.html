    Disclosure incentives

    Why do firms disclose anything?

    Market trust
    skeptical uncertain more confident
    1. 1
      Managers know more about the firm than investors
      Finance translation: information asymmetry
    2. 2
      Investors worry about what might be hidden
      Finance translation: hidden bad news → lower price / higher required return
    3. 3
      Disclosure reduces uncertainty → investors feel safer investing
      Finance translation: less mispricing → lower discount → lower cost of capital
    Example If investors fear undisclosed environmental fines, they hesitate. Clear disclosure reassures them.
    Disclosure is not altruism — it is a market response to distrust.
    The benchmark

    If disclosure were costless, what would firms do?

    Markets learn even from what is not said.

    Core logic
    1
    Withholding is itself a signal
    If investors expect you could speak, staying silent changes beliefs.
    2
    Silence gets interpreted pessimistically
    Rational investors ask: “What would make a manager choose not to disclose?”
    3
    Result: pressure toward (near) full disclosure
    As silence is punished, more firms disclose to avoid being pooled with worse firms.
    Key idea Silence is not neutral.
    What do we mean by “types”?
    Very good news
    Good news
    Average news
    Bad news
    Firms differ in the quality of information managers privately observe. As silence is punished, progressively worse firms choose to disclose.
    Unravelling intuition
    Step A
    Managers can disclose at zero cost
    Information is verifiable and easy to release.
    ↓
    Step B
    If you stay silent, investors infer “maybe bad”
    So silence lowers the market’s assessment.
    ↓
    Step C
    “Okay” firms disclose to avoid being lumped with “bad”
    That pushes the disclosure threshold upward…
    ↓
    Step D
    Benchmark outcome
    (Near) full disclosure — unless something makes disclosure costly.
    ★ Implication: selective disclosure needs frictions (costs, noise, litigation risk, proprietary costs).
    Benchmark takeaway: without frictions, markets punish silence → disclosure unravels toward completeness.
    Dye (2001) • Verrecchia (2001) • Milgrom (1981)
    From benchmark to reality

    Why isn’t everything disclosed?

    Because disclosure is costly, risky, and incentive-distorted.

    Benchmark (last slide)
    If disclosure were costless
    • Silence is interpreted as bad news
    • Markets punish non-disclosure
    • Disclosure unravels toward completeness
    ⇣
    Something must stop this logic.
    Frictions that stop unravelling
    1
    Measurement & verification costs
    Data are noisy, incomplete, or expensive to measure and assure.
    2
    Proprietary & competitive costs
    Disclosure can reveal strategy, margins, or operational weaknesses.
    3
    Legal & reputational liability
    What you disclose can be used against you ex post.
    4
    Short-term performance incentives
    Managers are rewarded for near-term results, not long-term transparency.
    Selective disclosure is not a bug — it is an equilibrium.
    This slide explains why the unravelling benchmark does not hold in practice. Each friction breaks the logic that silence must be punished.

    Definition

    Materiality

    A decision-focused filter (not a “nice-to-have” detail)

    Information is material if omitting, misstating or obscuring it could reasonably be expected to influence decisions that the primary users of general purpose financial reports make on the basis of those reports, which provide financial information about a specific reporting entity.

    Conceptual Framework for Financial Reporting IASB
    Meaning in one line
    Material = could change a user’s decision.
    –
    Omitting
    Leaving it out entirely.
    ≠
    Misstating
    Including it, but wrong (size, direction, timing).
    ∿
    Obscuring
    Burying it so users can’t see it clearly.
    The test is reasonable influence on primary users — not “is it interesting?”
    Emphasize: materiality is about decisions. Then stress the three verbs: omit, misstate, obscure. Use this to foreshadow why firms may strategically disclose: not only what they say, but how they present it.
    Now we name it • Decision filter

    What would change if materiality were taken seriously?

    Without a materiality lens
    “Compliance is enough”
    • Cultural heritage framed as “non-financial”
    • Decision made at operational level
    • Disclosure focuses on compliance
    • Incentives (cost, schedule, output) dominate silently
    With a materiality lens
    “This is decision-relevant risk”
    • Issue recognised as decision-relevant risk
    • Escalation to executives and board
    • Trade-offs explicitly discussed
    • Disclosure explains uncertainty & downside scenarios
    Why this matters
    What changes outside the firm
    • Investors reassess governance quality
    • Regulators reassess oversight
    • Future projects face higher scrutiny
    • “Social” issue becomes financially consequential
    Materiality does not tell firms what to do —
    it tells them what they are not allowed to ignore.
    Not ethics • not activism • not after-the-fact reporting — a decision filter under incentives and constraints.

    Why Materiality Matters Even More in Sustainability

    Sustainability creates a many-issues problem — materiality is what makes reporting usable.

    Sustainability issues are broad, multi-dimensional, and hard to measure

    Mini case — when “coincidence” becomes material

    Vodafone, Austerity & Tax Transparency

    Two events weren’t causally linked — but together they created a material legitimacy shock.

    Austerity announced: £6bn spending cuts
    ≈ 4 months later
    Private Eye claim goes viral: “£6bn tax avoided” (contested)
    What the public inferred
    1
    Same headline number, same year
    £6bn cuts + “£6bn avoided” felt like a direct trade-off.
    2
    Anger → mobilisation
    UK Uncut forms; high-street store actions & boycotts target Vodafone.
    3
    Dispute doesn’t stop the damage
    Even if the £6bn claim is contested, the reputational and political cost was real.
    ~£1.25bn
    reported HMRC settlement figure
    2012
    tax transparency report published
    So was tax transparency material?
    Materiality test (decision impact)
    Citizens / customers
    Trust & “license to operate”; boycott/brand switching.
    Investors
    Political/regulatory scrutiny; governance & cash-flow risk.
    Policymakers
    Pressure for reform; enforcement intensity.
    Even if tax paid didn’t change, transparency became decision-relevant because it affected trust, boycott risk, and political attention.
    Why telecoms?
    Intangibles & IP location make “where profits show up” especially salient.
    Take away: Materiality isn’t only about numbers — context + timing can turn a “soft” issue into a material one.

    Materiality depends on the decision-maker

    Material to whom? (financial vs impact materiality)
    Financial materiality (outside-in)
    Investors & lenders
    • Relevant to capital providers
    • Affects cash flows, risk, cost of capital
    • Time horizon: valuation and performance
    Lens: how sustainability impacts the firm
    Impact materiality (inside-out)
    Affected stakeholders
    • Relevant to people & communities
    • Concerns harm to people or the environment
    • Even if not immediately priced
    Lens: how the firm impacts the world
    The same sustainability issue may be material under one lens and not the other.

    Sustainability issues become material through:

    Greenwashing Emerges from Selective Disclosure

    Firms rarely need to lie to mislead.
    Influence often comes from what is omitted, obscured, or labelled immaterial.
    How it works
    –
    Omission
    Difficult impacts excluded (e.g., Scope 3, supply-chain harm).
    ∿
    Obscuring
    Aggregation, vague language, burying negatives in footnotes.
    ✓
    Selective metrics
    Highlight what improves; avoid what deteriorates or is hard to verify.
    ≋
    “Immaterial” labelling
    Justify silence by framing impacts as non-material or non-comparable.
    Result: stakeholders observe a curated version of reality.
    Why it’s rational
    Full transparency increases exposure
    Legal liability
    Regulatory scrutiny
    Investor reaction
    Reputational damage
    Managers face a trade-off
    Recognition increases accountability.
    Silence preserves flexibility.
    Many corporate net-zero commitments lack consistent, verifiable metrics — giving firms discretion in what they report and how progress is evaluated.
    Greenwashing is often not false disclosure — it is strategic non-disclosure.
    Incentives — regulation & enforcement

    When Does Greenwashing Become Rational?

    Greenwashing is most likely when the returns to appearing green exceed the costs of being misleading.

    Returns ↑ Strong demand for “green”
    Strong demand + Weak enforcement = Greenwashing risk
    Low detection & penalties Costs ↓
    Returns to Appearing Green ↑
    Consumer demand for sustainable products
    ESG-sensitive capital and screening
    Reputation benefits (employees, customers, partners)
    Competitive pressure (industry norms & peer claims)
    Looking green can create value — even before operations change.
    Costs of Misleading ↓
    Ambiguous standards (what “green” means is unclear)
    Limited verification (data gaps; weak assurance)
    Low regulatory penalties / slow enforcement
    Information asymmetry (outsiders can’t observe real effort)
    If no one can verify it, symbolic claims become attractive.
    Greenwashing is not primarily an ethics failure — it is an incentive outcome.
    Who reduces greenwashing?
    Regulators
    Clearer rules + credible enforcement
    Intermediaries
    Monitoring (media, NGOs, data providers, auditors)
    Firms
    Align incentives with real performance & verification

    Information gap

    TSMC × Taiwan Drought

    When a physical input constraint turns an “environmental issue” into a potential cash-flow risk
    What happened
    1
    Water looked operational
    Chip fabrication requires enormous volumes of ultra-pure water, but it was historically treated as a utilities issue rather than something investors price.
    2
    Severe drought hits Taiwan (2020–2021)
    Reservoir levels fell sharply and water restrictions were imposed, raising concern about global semiconductor supply. TSMC used about 156,000 metric tons of water per day across its science parks — roughly 60 Olympic-sized swimming pools.
    3
    Operational stress becomes visible
    Firms transported water by tanker trucks and increased conservation while signalling production continuity.
    4
    Reframing the risk
    Water scarcity began to be analysed as a supply-chain vulnerability — something investors may need to price.
    “Some sustainability risks become material through operations — not scandals.”
    Analytical takeaways
    A
    Materiality boundary shifts
    Water moves from “environmental” to financially relevant once it constrains production capacity.
    B
    Operational fragility is hard to observe
    Investors see revenue — not how dependent fabs are on local water infrastructure.
    C
    Incentives favour reassurance
    Firms often frame resource risk as “managed” until extreme events make the constraint verifiable.
    Physical constraint + high dependence + low substitutability → material risk
    Discussion: Is water risk primarily an ESG issue — or a core business continuity risk?
    Inputs that cannot be easily substituted are rarely “just operational.”
