    Topic 8
    Assurance, Governance, and Greenwashing

    Why assurance has become central to sustainability reporting

    Once sustainability information affects investment, financing, regulation, and public legitimacy, firms are no longer judged only on what they say, but on whether outsiders believe the numbers and the systems behind them.
    Four pressures pushing assurance demand
    1
    Capital-market pressure: investors increasingly treat sustainability information as decision-useful.
    2
    Regulatory pressure: mandatory reporting expands, so firms need evidence and process discipline.
    3
    Reputational pressure: unsupported claims now create litigation, enforcement, and media risk.
    4
    Internal management pressure: boards want systems that can survive challenge, not just glossy reporting.
    Core idea
    Assurance is not just about checking numbers. It helps shift sustainability reporting from persuasive narrative toward a more disciplined accountability system.
    • Without assurance, disclosure can drift toward cheap talk.
    • With assurance, some claims become harder to make carelessly.
    • Even so, boundaries, assumptions, and narrative framing still matter.
    Question: If a company says “net zero by 2040”, which parts of that statement can be verified today?
    Topic link
    Topic 7 asked how sustainability performance is measured; Topic 8 asks why anyone should trust the measures.

    What can be assured, and what remains more judgment-heavy

    The closer disclosure is to observed data and defined process, the easier it is to assure. The closer it is to strategy, ambition, and future narrative, the more judgment remains.
    More readily assured
    Data
    Energy use, injury rates, board composition, selected emissions, payroll diversity counts.
    Process
    Control ownership, approval workflows, data collection routines, evidence trails, methodology sign-off.
    Boundary
    Whether the firm used a stated reporting perimeter, definitions, and calculation method consistently.
    Harder to assure fully
    Narrative
    Claims such as “we embed sustainability into everything we do” are broad and often weakly falsifiable.
    Judgment
    Materiality decisions, scenario assumptions, transition-plan plausibility, and selective omission remain interpretive.
    Future
    Targets, pathways, and expected outcomes depend on assumptions that cannot be fully tested today.
    Main point: assurance can increase confidence, but it does not mean that every sentence in a sustainability report has been independently proven true.
    Reading focus
    A useful question is: what exactly is being assured: data, process, method, or the whole story?

    Limited assurance versus reasonable assurance

    The key distinction is not “good versus bad”, but the degree of comfort, the depth of procedures, and the level of residual uncertainty left to users.
    Limited assurance
    Scope
    More constrained procedures, often focused on plausibility, inquiry, analytics, and selected testing.
    Use
    Common starting point in early-stage sustainability reporting where systems are still maturing.
    Signal
    “Nothing has come to our attention” type conclusion — useful, but not equivalent to a financial statement audit opinion.
    Reasonable assurance
    Scope
    Deeper evidence gathering and stronger basis for a positive conclusion.
    Use
    More demanding for firms because it requires stronger controls, documentation, and repeatable systems.
    Signal
    Greater confidence for users, but still not absolute certainty and still sensitive to definitions and assumptions.
    Why this matters now
    The live policy debate is not whether assurance matters, but how far assurance obligations should go and how quickly firms can realistically build the underlying control infrastructure.
    Main point
    Limited assurance can improve credibility while still leaving substantial room for managerial discretion.

    External assurance only works if internal controls work first

    Assurance sits at the end of a reporting chain. If source systems are weak, accountability is vague, or data ownership is unclear, the assurance statement may look formal while resting on fragile foundations.
    A simple internal-control architecture
    Owner
    Every metric needs a named owner responsible for evidence quality and methodological consistency.
    Evidence
    Documentation, source files, assumptions, exceptions, and boundary decisions need to be retained.
    Review
    Management review should challenge anomalies, not merely approve outputs.
    Escalation
    Material issues need a route to audit committee or board oversight.
    Where sustainability controls often fail
    • Spreadsheet dependence with low version control.
    • Boundary confusion over subsidiaries, contractors, and value-chain data.
    • Weak integration between sustainability teams and finance, legal, operations, and risk.
    • Late-stage verification instead of control-by-design.
    Question: Which is more dangerous: inaccurate data, or accurate data embedded in a misleading boundary or narrative?
    Main point
    Assurance does not replace governance; it relies on governance.

    Governance determines whether bad news can travel upward

    The formal existence of committees and policies is less important than whether the reporting system can surface uncomfortable information and force challenge.
    Governance chain
    Management
    Chooses targets, reporting boundary, metrics, and narrative tone.
    Sustainability team
    Coordinates data gathering and reporting design, but often depends on other functions for evidence.
    Finance / risk / internal audit
    Can professionalise control discipline and challenge inconsistencies.
    Audit committee / board
    Should ask whether reported progress is matched by robust systems, incentives, and escalation mechanisms.
    Questions to ask
    • Who owns the metric?
    • Who challenges optimistic narrative?
    • Who sees exceptions and restatements?
    • Are incentives aligned with real sustainability outcomes or with presentational success?
    A report can be beautifully designed and still reveal weak governance if no one appears accountable for difficult judgments.
    Governance
    Good governance is not simply the presence of oversight structures; it is the presence of credible challenge.

    How the current assurance landscape emerged

    A useful way to read this topic is as a progression: from voluntary reporting, to framework-building, to mandatory standards, to assurance and anti-greenwashing enforcement.
    Historical progression
    Voluntary era
    CSR and sustainability reports were narrative-heavy, heterogeneous, and often weakly verified.
    Framework era
    GRI, SASB, and TCFD made disclosure more structured and comparable.
    Standards era
    ISSB and ESRS moved sustainability reporting toward formal standard-setting.
    Assurance era
    CSRD, emerging assurance expectations, and ISSA 5000 pushed credibility and auditability to the centre.
    Current developments
    • ISSA 5000 gives the market a dedicated global sustainability assurance standard.
    • EU Omnibus debate shows that the political question is now scope, burden, and the pace of assurance escalation.
    • Anti-greenwashing rules increasingly police claims outside the annual report too: marketing, fund names, and product language.
    Coming next: the next two slides show what ISSA 5000 does in practice, and why the Omnibus debate matters for assurance expectations.
    Shift
    The field has moved from “should firms report?” to “what must be reported, with what assurance, and with what consequences?”

    ISSA 5000: what the global assurance standard is trying to do

    ISSA 5000 matters because it creates a common assurance architecture for sustainability information without dictating the sustainability metrics themselves.
    What the standard does
    Coverage
    It is designed for sustainability assurance engagements across different topics and reporting frameworks, not just climate disclosures.
    Users
    IAASB says it is designed for use by both professional accountants and non-accountant assurance practitioners.
    Assurance
    The standard supports both limited assurance and reasonable assurance, and can accommodate mixed engagements across different disclosures.
    Adoption
    Jurisdictional adoption is now underway, with many adoptions aligning with periods beginning on or after 15 December 2026.
    What it does not solve
    • It does not decide what a company must disclose; that still depends on laws and reporting standards such as ESRS or ISSB.
    • It does not make targets automatically credible; future pathways still depend on assumptions, boundaries, and omitted context.
    • It does not remove judgment; materiality, estimation uncertainty, and narrative wording still need challenge.
    • It does not turn every sustainability sentence into an audited fact; the assurance scope still has to be specified carefully.
    A stronger assurance standard can improve confidence in the work, but it cannot rescue a weak claim.
    Main point: ISSA 5000 strengthens the assurance process. It does not replace the need for better disclosure design, evidence, and governance.
    Sources
    IAASB overview of ISSA 5000, the ISSA 5000 standard, and IAASB adoption details updated February 2026.

    Why the EU Omnibus debate matters for Topic 8

    The policy question is no longer whether sustainability reporting exists; it is how wide the scope should be, how burdensome the system should be, and how quickly assurance should intensify.
    What the Commission proposed
    26 Feb 2025
    The European Commission adopted a simplification package aimed at cutting compliance burden while keeping the largest companies in scope.
    Scope
    The Commission said the package would remove around 80% of companies from CSRD scope, focusing on firms with more than 1,000 employees and specified turnover or balance-sheet thresholds.
    Standards
    It proposed revising and simplifying ESRS and removing the requirement for sector-specific standards.
    Assurance
    It proposed keeping assurance at limited assurance, rather than moving later to reasonable assurance.
    Why this matters
    • It shows assurance is political as well as technical: the debate is about cost, pace, feasibility, and competitiveness, not only credibility.
    • It affects expectations: if limited assurance remains the practical ceiling for longer, users may still face substantial residual uncertainty.
    • It sits alongside anti-greenwashing pressure: outside the annual report, claims are also being policed by rules such as the Financial Conduct Authority (FCA) anti-greenwashing rule, the Competition and Markets Authority (CMA) Green Claims Code, and European fund-name guidance.
    • It is a live policy debate: the direction of travel is clear, but the final scope and burden are still contested.
    Topic 8 is not only about assurance technique. It is also about who should bear the cost of making sustainability claims more credible.
    Question: If assurance improves trust but imposes real cost, where should regulators draw the line?
    Sources
    European Commission Omnibus package page, Financial Conduct Authority FG24/3, and the Competition and Markets Authority Green Claims Code.

    Greenwashing is not one thing

    A typology helps move the discussion beyond moral language and toward diagnosis: what exactly is misleading, and through which mechanism?
    Four recurring forms
    Claim
    Claim inflation
    Headline wording overstates environmental or social progress relative to the underlying mechanism.
    Boundary
    Selective omission
    Operations, suppliers, geographies, or time periods are left out because they would weaken the story.
    Metric
    Method manipulation
    Definitions, baselines, or denominators shift in ways that make performance appear stronger.
    Halo
    Assurance halo
    Assurance over a narrow set of metrics makes the wider report feel more trustworthy than it really is.
    How to test a claim
    • What is the headline claim?
    • What evidence supports it?
    • What conditions or boundaries are missing?
    • What is actually assured, and what is just narrated?
    • Would a reasonable reader take away a stronger message than the evidence supports?
    A practical test: start with the headline, then test the evidence, the missing conditions, and the likely reader takeaway.
    Greenwashing is often less about a false sentence and more about a misleading overall impression.
    Main point
    The aim is to classify the type of greenwashing risk, not just notice that a report feels vague.

    Delta’s “fully carbon neutral” announcement

    The clip presents the claim in the company’s own words before the later evidence and challenge are examined.
    What to listen for
    • Headline wording: how strong does “fully carbon neutral” sound to an ordinary listener?
    • Mechanism: what explanation is given for how the claim would be achieved?
    • User takeaway: what impression might a consumer or investor form after hearing the interview?
    Question: Before looking at the later reporting and legal challenge, ask whether the wording sounds broader than the mechanism behind it.

    Case 1 — Delta Air Lines: “carbon neutral” or carbon narrative?

    Delta is a strong Topic 8 case because the headline claim was bold, the sector is hard to abate, and later disclosures softened some of the language around the pathway.
    Evidence trail
    Headline claim: “carbon neutral”
    Feb 2020
    Delta announced a $1 billion plan to become the first carbon neutral airline globally.
    May 2023
    A consumer class action challenged the campaign, arguing that the neutrality claim depended on problematic offsets.
    May 2025
    Delta’s 2024 report still described net-zero 2050 as a goal, with 13M gallons of sustainable aviation fuel (SAF) delivered and 44M gallons of jet fuel saved in 2024.
    Apr 2026
    Reuters/Bloomberg reported that Delta dropped its 10% SAF by 2030 target and reframed net-zero 2050 as an aspiration.
    Sources: Delta 2020 announcement; Delta Difference Report 2024; Reuters/Bloomberg, 14 April 2026.
    Where the risk appears
    • Absolute wording: “carbon neutral” is stronger than “reducing emissions over time.”
    • Mechanism risk: offsets and future technologies are not the same as cutting current flight emissions.
    • Credibility signal: later target rollback does not prove deception, but it does justify rechecking the original claim.
    • Governance question: who should have challenged whether the headline matched the evidence?
    Question: Does later target rollback show greenwashing, or does it mainly show how difficult transition planning is in hard-to-abate sectors?
    When the headline is stronger than the mechanism, the greenwashing risk rises sharply.
    Why it matters
    Delta is useful because it shows how a sophisticated report can still face scrutiny if the public-facing claim appears broader than the operational reality.

    Case 2 — Keurig Canada: recyclable in theory, not in practice

    Keurig is a very different greenwashing case: here the issue is not a corporate transition claim, but a product-level environmental claim that omitted material conditions about how recycling systems actually work.
    Evidence trail
    Headline claim: “recyclable”
    Dec 2020
    Keurig Dr Pepper announced that all K-Cup pods it produces are made from recyclable polypropylene #5 plastic.
    Jan 2022
    Canada’s Competition Bureau said the claims were misleading; outside British Columbia and Quebec, pods were not widely accepted in municipal recycling programs.
    Jan 2022
    Keurig Canada agreed to a CA$3 million penalty, an CA$800,000 environmental donation, and corrective notices.
    Sep 2024
    The SEC said Keurig’s 2019 and 2020 reports omitted concerns from two large U.S. recyclers; the company paid a $1.5 million penalty.
    Sources: Keurig/KDP product claims; Competition Bureau Canada, 6 January 2022; SEC, 10 September 2024; Keurig Canada corrective notice.
    Where the risk appears
    • Technical truth vs practical truth: a pod can be made from recyclable plastic and still not be meaningfully recyclable for most users.
    • Material omission: local collection, sorting, and processing conditions were crucial context.
    • Governance failure: marketing, packaging, sustainability, legal, and operations all needed to challenge the real-world claim.
    • Assurance lesson: checking material specification is not enough; the claim also has to match actual system performance.
    Question: How would you rewrite the claim so it stays positive but no longer overstates what an ordinary consumer can realistically do?
    A claim can be technically defensible and still be misleading overall.
    Why it matters
    Keurig shows that greenwashing can arise through omitted conditions and unrealistic consumer interpretation, not only through false data.

    Case 3 — HSBC climate adverts: positive messages, missing context

    HSBC adds a very different kind of greenwashing risk to Topic 8: visually polished climate advertising that highlighted real initiatives, but omitted material context about the bank’s wider financed-emissions exposure.
    Evidence trail
    Headline claim: climate-positive bank narrative
    Oct 2021
    HSBC posters said “Climate change doesn’t do borders” and highlighted up to $1 trillion of transition finance and support for planting 2 million trees.
    Oct 2022
    The Advertising Standards Authority (ASA) upheld complaints, saying the adverts omitted significant information about HSBC’s contribution to greenhouse-gas emissions through the businesses it financed.
    ASA finding
    The ruling said consumers would likely take away a positive overall environmental impression, strengthened by the climate imagery and the COP26 context.
    Missing context
    The ASA pointed to HSBC’s own reporting on large financed emissions and its intention to continue some thermal-coal financing for years into the future.
    Source: Advertising Standards Authority ruling on HSBC UK Bank plc, 19 October 2022.
    Where the risk appears
    • Omission greenwashing: the issue was not necessarily that each statement was false, but that the overall advert left out material counter-context.
    • Halo effect: elegant visuals and morally serious wording made the ad feel more environmentally responsible than the wider business picture supported.
    • Finance and lending: greenwashing can arise in financial-sector advertising, not only in product marketing or manufacturing claims.
    • Governance question: who should challenge an advert that is factually selective but potentially misleading overall?
    Question: Can an advert be misleading even if the statements it highlights are individually true?
    This case shows how omission, imagery, and timing can shape a misleading overall impression.
    Why it matters
    HSBC shows how selective truth can mislead when a high-level climate message is not balanced by the wider emissions context.

    Which of these looks like greenwashing?

    The aim is not cynicism. It is disciplined discrimination: some claims are classic risk signals, some need much more evidence, and some may be relatively credible because they are specific, bounded, and more falsifiable.
    HSBC
    Likely risk
    “Climate change doesn’t do borders.”
    Omission risk
    Powerful climate framing, but the Advertising Standards Authority said the advert omitted material context about financed emissions and fossil-fuel exposure.
    Ryanair
    Likely risk
    “Europe’s Lowest Emissions Airline”
    Comparative risk
    A bold superlative in a high-emissions sector can turn a relative efficiency claim into an overly green overall impression.
    Shell
    Likely risk
    “Drive carbon-neutral”
    Offsetting risk
    The phrase gives immediate reassurance, but the Advertising Standards Authority warned that the mechanism and conditions were not made clear enough.
    KLM
    Likely risk
    “Fly Responsibly”
    Virtue-language risk
    The slogan sounds morally mature, but later legal challenge focused on whether the campaign painted too rosy a picture.
    Oatly
    Potentially credible
    Climate footprint label in kg CO2e/kg
    Quantified disclosure
    More specific, measurable, and easier to challenge. It still depends on scope and method, but it is not vague virtue language.
    Generic packaging
    Needs evidence
    “100% recyclable”
    System-context risk
    The key question is: recyclable where, under what collection and sorting conditions, and for how many actual consumers?
    How to evaluate the wall
    • Sort each tile into likely greenwashing risk, needs more evidence, or potentially credible disclosure.
    • Then ask what exact evidence, boundary, or caveat would be needed before trusting the claim.
    • This builds pattern recognition without assuming every sustainability message is automatically deceptive.
    Not every green-looking claim is greenwashing. The real skill is knowing which claims deserve immediate skepticism and which deserve closer verification.
    Sources
    Advertising Standards Authority materials on HSBC, Ryanair, and Shell; Dutch court/KLM reporting; and Oatly’s climate-footprint disclosure page.

    Topic 8 summary and key takeaways

    The central theme is simple: assurance can strengthen credibility, but only when claims, boundaries, assumptions, and governance are all aligned.
    What to remember
    • Assurance is a credibility mechanism, not a cure-all.
    • Internal controls and governance determine whether assurance rests on strong foundations.
    • Greenwashing often works through omission, framing, and overconfident wording, not just false numbers.
    • A key skill is to separate assured data from persuasive narrative.
    The right question is not only “is this metric correct?” but also “could this wording mislead a reasonable reader?”
    Looking ahead
    • Delta, Keurig, and HSBC contrast overstated ambition, omitted practical conditions, and omission-driven advertising.
    • The suspicion wall contrasts vague virtue language with more bounded, falsifiable disclosure.
    • Suggested readings: Al-Shaer & Zaman, review pieces on sustainability assurance, and recent company reports with assurance statements.
    Topic 9: once disclosures leave the firm, intermediaries turn them into ratings, scores, and market signals, often with further interpretation.
    Closing point
    Strong sustainability communication depends not only on better data, but on better claim governance.
