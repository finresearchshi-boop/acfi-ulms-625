    Topic 3
    Sustainability reporting frameworks and Standards

    Why do we need sustainability reporting standards?

    Because without common definitions and (some) verification, sustainability disclosure becomes cheap talk.
    1) Materiality selects what matters
    Which issues could reasonably influence primary users’ decisions?
    2) Standards define how to report
    Metrics, boundaries, assumptions, and (sometimes) assurance.
    3) Evidence: regulation raises the floor
    Mandatory/structured disclosure tends to increase volume and improve comparability — but managerial discretion does not disappear.
    Before
    • Voluntary, heterogeneous
    • Selective emphasis on “good news”
    • Hard for investors to compare firms
    →
    After
    • Baseline disclosure requirements
    • More structure and consistency
    • Greater comparability (not perfect)
    Key idea: standards don’t remove incentives — they change what firms must put “on the record”.

    Comparability aim
    Moves reporting toward common categories (E, S, HR, anti-corruption, etc.) and enables more cross-firm comparison.
    UK — TCFD-aligned pathway

    2021 →

    Short takeaway: standards raise the floor — but firms still choose framing, scope, and emphasis.

    Sustainability Reporting Is Following the Path of Financial Reporting

    “Capital markets cannot function well without a shared reporting language.”
    Financial reporting (then → now)
    Institutional development
    Before modern standards
    • Firms chose their own metrics
    • Limited comparability
    • High information asymmetry
    Over time
    Markets demanded structure → Standard setters emerged → US GAAP and IFRS created a common reporting language
    Trajectory (conceptual)
    Voluntary Fragmented Standardized
    Key bridge
    Financially material information does not remain unstructured for long.
    Standardisation improves comparability — but judgment never disappears.
    Sustainability reporting (now)
    Convergence pressure
    Today
    • Multiple frameworks
    • Measurement challenges
    • Limited comparability
    • High discretion
    What’s changing
    Markets are now demanding convergence.
    ISSB is attempting to do for sustainability what IFRS did for financial reporting.
    Trajectory (conceptual)
    Voluntary Fragmented (moving toward standardization)
    Standardisation is what transforms disclosure into decision-useful information.
    Discussion: Why did IFRS emerge? Global investors, cross-border capital, comparability…
    Now: sustainability risks are priced globally — so the same pressure is emerging.

    Frameworks vs Standards — Not the Same Thing

    Before we talk about sustainability rules, we must separate two ideas students often confuse.
    A simple analogy
    Framework
    The Highway Code
    Principles for safe driving
    ≠
    Standard
    The Speed Limit
    An exact number you must follow
    Frameworks
    “How to think”
    What they do
    • Provide structure for analysis & decision-making
    • Help identify what topics matter and why
    • Guide governance and risk thinking
    Key idea
    Frameworks guide judgment — they do not prescribe exact numbers.
    Frameworks create consistency in thinking.
    Standards
    “What exactly to report”
    What they specify
    • Required metrics and definitions
    • Boundaries, assumptions, measurement rules
    • Sometimes assurance requirements
    Key idea
    Standards reduce discretion by making disclosure more specific.
    Standards create consistency in reporting.
    Example: TCFD
    Framework
    TCFD — Task Force on Climate-related Financial Disclosures. It organizes climate disclosure into four simple questions:
    1
    Who oversees climate risk?
    2
    How could it affect the business?
    3
    How is risk managed?
    4
    How is performance measured?
    Mini check: Does this tell firms how to think, or does it force them to report exact numbers?
    → Exactly — it is a framework. Frameworks often come first; standards usually follow.
    Frameworks shape analysis. • Standards shape disclosure.
    Typical evolution
    Framework → Standard → Assurance → Enforcement
    Today: sustainability is moving from frameworks toward standards.

    How did we end up with so many systems?

    Different users wanted different things — so different institutions emerged.
    A short institutional timeline
    Impacts / broader stakeholders Investor-focused / enterprise value Regime / enforcement shift
    1. 1997
      GRI founded
      Why: legitimacy + impacts (“what is our footprint?”)
    2. 2011
      SASB founded
      Why: investor decision-usefulness + industry comparability (“what matters financially?”)
    3. 2017
      TCFD recommendations
      Why: climate risk becomes finance-relevant; common structure for governance/strategy/risk/metrics
    4. 2021
      Value Reporting Foundation (SASB + IIRC)
      Why: simplify enterprise-value reporting ecosystem; reduce duplication
    5. Nov 2021
      ISSB announced (COP26)
      Why: global baseline for investor-focused sustainability disclosure
    6. 2022
      VRF + CDSB consolidated into IFRS Foundation
      Why: institutional “plumbing” + resources move under IFRS to build ISSB standards
    7. Jun 2023
      IFRS S1 / S2 issued (effective 1 Jan 2024)
      Why: first global baseline standards for sustainability + climate disclosures
    8. Jul 2023
      ESRS adopted; FY2024 first CSRD year (reports in 2025)
      Why: EU double materiality + enforceable regime (assurance + scope expansion)
    Why it fragmented
    Two purposes
    Impacts
    Broader stakeholder outcomes
    (double materiality logic)
    Enterprise value
    Investor decision-usefulness
    (baseline comparability)
    Enforcement regimes
    Voluntary → semi-mandatory → mandatory + assurance
    Discussion questions
    Who is the primary user of this information — investors, or society?
    Is it voluntary, or enforceable?
    👉 Fragmentation wasn’t an accident — it reflected different purposes (impacts vs enterprise value) and different enforcement regimes.

    GRI asks a different question: “What do you do to the world?”

    Impact materiality starts with societal consequences — not investor decisions.

    Impact-first logic
    • Begins with the firm’s external effects on people and the environment
    • Stakeholders include workers, communities, ecosystems — not only capital providers
    • Materiality is triggered by severity of harm, even if markets do not price it immediately
    • Scope spans environmental, social, and governance impacts across the value chain
    “Under GRI, an issue can be material even if investors never react.”
    Paradigm shift: materiality is not defined by market response.
    Micro-contrast
    Investor lens asks:
    Does this affect cash flows?
    GRI lens asks:
    Does this affect people?
    Concrete example
    Living Wage in Global Retail Supply Chains
    Scenario

    A multinational retailer sources garments from factories where workers earn below a living wage.

    GRI logic asks the firm to disclose:
    • Wage levels vs living-wage benchmarks
    • Working conditions
    • Supplier audits
    • Remediation efforts
    Not because profits are immediately affected…
    …but because the firm contributes to a societal outcome.
    Key question
    “The question is not ‘Does this move the share price tomorrow?’
    The question is ‘Are we contributing to harm?’”
    In-class prompt
    “If investors ignored this issue completely, would it still deserve disclosure?”
    GRI’s answer is yes.
    Evidence anchor Founded in 1997 to create a global common language for organisations’ impacts on society.
    Impact materiality widens the boundary of corporate accountability.

    SASB asks: “What changes valuation in this industry?”

    Financial materiality focuses on information that affects enterprise value and investor decisions.

    Investor-first logic (financial materiality)
    • Focuses on capital providers (shareholders, lenders, analysts)
    • Material if it could affect cash flows, risk, or cost of capital
    • Filters disclosure to decision-useful information
    • Not all sustainability issues are included — only those linked to valuation
    “SASB is not asking what is important to society — it asks what is important to investors.”
    Key SASB innovation — Industry specificity

    Material sustainability risks differ across industries.

    “Same standardised structure — different metrics by industry.”
    Concrete examples
    Airlines
    • Fuel efficiency & emissions intensity
    • Carbon regulation → future cost risk
    • Fuel cost → operating margins
    Carbon emissions matter here not as ethics — but as operating cost and regulatory risk.
    Banks
    • Financed emissions & climate risk exposure
    • Loan portfolio exposure
    • Credit risk under transition
    For banks, the sustainability risk is not their own emissions — it is the emissions of the firms they lend to.
    GRI vs SASB
    GRI SASB
    Core question What harm do we cause? What affects firm value?
    Users Society / stakeholders Investors
    Scope Broad impacts Decision-relevant metrics
    Created in 2011 to help investors compare financially material sustainability risks across industries.
    Financial materiality narrows disclosure to what changes investment decisions.

    ISSB: Making Sustainability Disclosure Look Like Financial Reporting

    A global investor baseline for sustainability information

    What the ISSB is trying to do
    Purpose
    • Create globally comparable sustainability disclosures
    • Integrate sustainability into capital markets decision-making
    • Reduce reporting fragmentation across jurisdictions
    Design philosophy
    • Same logic as financial reporting
    • Focus on enterprise value
    • Information relevant to investors and lenders
    ISSB treats sustainability as financially relevant information, not CSR communication.
    IFRS S1 — General Sustainability
    • Identify sustainability risks & opportunities
    • Impact on strategy and financial position
    • Link to financial statements and future performance
    S1 = all sustainability risks that could affect the business
    IFRS S2 — Climate (TCFD-Aligned)
    Governance
    Strategy
    Risk management
    Metrics & targets
    S2 turns climate risk into something auditors and investors can analyze.
    Financial statements → already audited ISSB disclosures → designed to connect to them
    Why it exists
    Before ISSB
    • Multiple frameworks
    • Inconsistent metrics
    • Firms chose what to disclose
    Investors faced a comparability problem.
    ISSB response
    A global baseline

    Countries can add more (e.g., ESRS), but everyone should disclose at least this minimum.

    GRI
    Broad accountability to society
    ISSB
    Decision-useful information for investors
    Issued June 2023 • Effective 1 Jan 2024 • Built on SASB + TCFD + CDSB
    ISSB does not end the ecosystem — it organizes it.

    TCFD: The Climate Disclosure Skeleton

    The first globally adopted structure for sustainability risk reporting

    What TCFD actually is
    NOT a standard
    TCFD does not prescribe exact metrics.
    It tells firms how to organise climate information so investors can analyse risk.
    • No fixed KPIs
    • No single emissions threshold
    • No specific template
    “TCFD doesn’t tell firms what number to report — it tells them how to explain climate risk.”
    The TCFD 4-Pillar Framework
    1. Governance
    Who oversees climate risk?
    • Board responsibility
    • Management accountability
    2. Strategy
    How climate affects the business model
    • Scenario analysis
    • Long-term resilience
    3. Risk Management
    How risks are identified & managed
    • Integrated into ERM
    4. Metrics & Targets
    How exposure is measured
    • Emissions
    • Targets & indicators
    “TCFD translated climate change into financial risk language.”
    Why climate became the special case
    Three reasons
    1. Economy-wide exposure — every industry is affected
    2. Forward-looking risk — future cash flows (not just past performance)
    3. Quantifiable channels — transition & physical risk can be analysed
    Physical risk
    Damage from extreme weather, heat, flooding, sea level rise
    Transition risk
    Policy, carbon pricing, technology shifts, stranded assets
    Concrete example: a utility company
    Physical risk
    • Power plants exposed to flooding
    • Heat reduces grid efficiency
    Transition risk
    • Carbon pricing increases operating cost
    • Coal plants become stranded assets
    → Investors care because future profits change.
    One-page “TCFD note” (structure)
    Governance: board climate committee
    Strategy: 2°C scenario analysis
    Risk: integrated into enterprise risk
    Metrics: Scope 1 & 2, reduction targets
    Why TCFD matters today
    Most modern climate disclosure rules are built on the TCFD architecture.
    ISSB (IFRS S2) EU ESRS (climate) National regulators
    How the ecosystem fits together
    SystemWhat it provides
    SASBIndustry metrics
    GRIImpact disclosure
    TCFDRisk reporting structure
    ISSBFormal standard
    TCFD recommendations published: 2017 • Adopted widely by regulators and investors
    Climate reporting came first because it could be translated into financial risk.

    EU Approach: Double Materiality with Enforcement

    Accountability to society and information for capital markets

    What the EU is trying to do
    Core logic
    • a corporate accountability system
    • a transparency mechanism
    • a policy instrument
    The EU does not treat sustainability reporting only as a capital-market information problem.
    Mandatory regime (CSRD)
    • Legally required disclosures
    • Applies to large companies (listed SMEs later)
    • Standardised format; filed in annual reports
    Under CSRD, sustainability disclosure becomes part of corporate reporting law — not voluntary communication.
    Assurance / enforcement
    Limited assurance required
    Implication: disclosure ≠ marketing • disclosure ≈ regulated reporting
    The heart of ESRS: Double materiality
    Double Materiality = Two Questions
    1) Outside-in (Financial materiality)
    How sustainability affects the firm
    → risks, profitability, valuation
    2) Inside-out (Impact materiality)
    How the firm affects society & the environment
    → emissions, workers, communities, biodiversity
    Society
    Society → Firm (risk) →
    ← Firm → Society (impact)
    Firm
    ISSB asks what affects firm value. ESRS asks both what affects firm value and what the firm affects.
    Process transparency (unique)
    • Document the materiality assessment
    • Justify included / excluded topics
    • Disclose methodology
    Companies must now explain why an issue is not material.
    ESRS adopted: 31 Jul 2023 CSRD applies: FY2024 → reports in 2025
    Key contrast with ISSB
    ISSBESRS
    Investor baselineSocietal accountability + investors
    Financial materialityDouble materiality
    Global comparabilityRegulatory enforcement
    Minimum disclosureBroad ESG coverage
    “ISSB starts from investors. ESRS starts from society and investors.”
    Why this matters
    • More disclosure topics
    • Higher compliance cost
    • Less discretion for firms
    • Reduced selective disclosure
    → harder to greenwash
    CSRD turns sustainability reporting from communication into regulated disclosure.

    When Does Greenwashing Become Rational?

    Disclosure is shaped by incentives, not only by ethics

    The greenwashing incentive condition
    Incentive side
    Information / enforcement side
    Returns to appearing green ↑
    • Access to capital
    • Consumer demand
    • Reputation benefits
    • ESG ratings & marketing value
    Costs of misleading ↓
    • Weak verification
    • Vague standards
    • Low penalties
    • Limited auditing
    High demand for ESG + Low verification → Greenwashing risk
    Delmas & Burbano (2011)
    Simplified definition
    Greenwashing = positive environmental communication
    without corresponding environmental performance
    2×2 typology
    Communicates a lot
    Communicates little
    Good
    performance
    Poor
    performance
    Genuine sustainability
    “do & tell”
    Greenhushing
    Greenwashing
    “tell without doing”
    Silent brown firm
    “Greenwashing is not talking — it is talking without doing.”
    Greenwashing is an information strategy under imperfect verification.
    Why firms greenwash (3 drivers)
    1) External pressures
    • Consumer demand for green firms
    • Investor ESG demand
    • NGO / media attention
    • Weak regulation
    Interpretation: firms are rewarded for appearing responsible.
    2) Organisational factors
    • Marketing incentives > operational change
    • PR–operations misalignment
    • Capability gaps
    “It is easier to change messaging than to change production.”
    3) Managerial behaviour
    • Short-termism
    • Optimism bias (“we won’t get caught”)
    • Narrow framing
    Managers optimise the visible metric.
    Mechanism (the real insight)
    Greenwashing is most likely when benefits of appearing green are high and the probability of detection is low.
    Why materiality matters
    Materiality determines what must be disclosed and what can be omitted.
    → Greenwashing often occurs through materiality judgments.
    What it is (and isn’t)
    Usually NOT
    Outright false statements
    Often IS
    Omission • vagueness • selective metrics • “not material”
    Link to regulation
    Standards and enforcement raise the cost of misleading disclosure:
    ISSB → comparability ESRS → accountability Assurance → detection

    When ESG Marketing Exceeds ESG Practice

    A real case from asset management

    The company
    DWS Group
    Majority owned by Deutsche Bank
    Business model
    • Manages investment funds
    • Markets ESG investment products
    • Sells credibility and trust
    Asset managers do not sell products — they sell information and judgement.
    What investors were told
    • ESG integral to investment process
    • Embedded across portfolios
    • Positioned as an ESG leader
    Investors believed ESG factors were systematically used in investment decisions.
    The allegation
    Whistleblower (2021)
    • ESG integration overstated
    • Some ESG-labelled assets not screened
    • Internal systems weaker than claims
    Communication > Implementation
    Positive sustainability communication without matching practice
    Textbook greenwashing (Delmas & Burbano)
    Key teaching insight
    This was greenwashing via disclosure — not via pollution.
    Regulatory response
    Investigations
    • U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission
    • German regulator (BaFin)
    Regulators examined disclosure accuracy, not environmental damage.
    Outcome (2023)
    ≈ $25 million penalty
    Misleading ESG integration statements
    Why this matters
    • ESG affects fund flows
    • Capital allocation depends on disclosure
    • Therefore ESG becomes financial information
    Why standards exist
    • No consistent ESG definitions
    • No standard metrics
    • Weak verification
    ISSB ESRS Assurance
    Finance firms can greenwash through words, not factories.

    Why standards change behaviour (not just disclosure)

    Standards work by altering incentives, not by making firms ethical.

    1
    Specific metrics → less storytelling
    Without standards
    • Performance described with language
    • Words are flexible (“committed”, “aligned”, “responsible”)
    What standards do
    • Defined indicators
    • Calculation methods
    • Scope definitions
    Scope 1/2/3 (not “low carbon”)
    Injury rate (not “we prioritise safety”)
    Financed emissions (not “green portfolio”)
    Numbers restrict narrative freedom.
    2
    Comparability → markets can judge
    Before standards
    • Each company reported differently
    • Investors couldn’t compare
    • Bad firms could hide inside customised disclosure
    What standards do
    • Same definitions
    • Same boundaries
    • Same units
    Example
    Two airlines: 75g vs 110g CO₂ / passenger-km
    No marketing can hide that.
    Comparability turns disclosure into competition.
    3
    Verification → misleading becomes costly
    Previously
    • ESG claims rarely audited
    • Penalties unclear
    • Reputational risk uncertain
    What standards enable
    • Assurance (auditors)
    • Regulatory review
    • Legal liability
    DWS: the issue was not pollution — it was misleading disclosure.
    Verification converts PR risk into legal risk.
    Firms always face incentives. Standards increase the cost of misrepresentation.
    “Standards don’t remove incentives — they change the payoff matrix.”

    Comparability vs Relevance: the unavoidable tension

    Why sustainability reporting will never feel perfectly satisfying.

    Relevance
    Decision usefulness for this specific firm
    What it looks like
    • Firm-specific disclosures
    • Management judgement
    • Narrative explanations
    • Forward-looking discussion
    What it gets right
    • Captures real risks
    • Reflects the business model
    • Useful for understanding strategy
    What goes wrong
    • Hard to compare across firms
    • Selective emphasis
    • Easier to spin
    Flexible disclosure tells you a lot about one firm — but little about how it compares.
    Comparability
    Same information across firms
    What it looks like
    • Defined metrics
    • Standard definitions
    • Required tables
    • Common templates
    What it gets right
    • Benchmarking
    • Screening
    • Rankings & portfolios
    What goes wrong
    • Box-ticking behaviour
    • Superficial compliance
    • Important nuances lost
    Standardised disclosure lets you compare firms — but may miss what actually matters.
    More flexibility → more relevance, less comparability | More standardisation → more comparability, less nuance
    “You cannot maximise both at the same time.”
    Example: Scope 3 emissions
    Scope 3 = emissions from suppliers and customers (not the company’s own factories)
    If we require strict numbers
    • Firms estimate supplier/customer emissions
    • Numbers can be unreliable
    • But outputs become comparable
    If we allow narrative discussion
    • Firms explain real exposure
    • But impossible to benchmark
    • Easier to exaggerate or hide
    Comparable numbers are imperfect — but pure narrative is manipulable.

    Why more ESG data does not automatically create agreement

    More reporting ≠ one objective measure of sustainability

    What we expect
    “If companies disclose more sustainability information, ratings should converge.”
    Intuitive logic
    More disclosure
    →
    More data
    →
    Less uncertainty
    →
    Similar ratings
    But empirically, this does not happen. Agencies can read the same disclosures and still disagree.
    Analogy
    In finance, analysts using the same financial statements usually reach roughly similar valuations. In ESG, the same disclosures can still produce very different ratings.
    Evidence
    Correlation across ESG ratings
    ≈ 0.4 – 0.7
    Systematic disagreement
    Correlation across credit ratings
    ≈ 0.99
    Near-consensus
    Two agencies can rate the same firm as an ESG leader and ESG average at the same time. This is not just noise.
    Academic anchor
    Berg, Koelbel & Rigobon (2022)
    “Aggregate Confusion: The Divergence of ESG Ratings”
    Reveiw of Finance
    Their decomposition
    SourceShare of disagreement
    Measurement56%
    Scope38%
    Weighting6%
    Why ratings still diverge
    1) Scope differences — what is ESG?
    • Different topics included (e.g., lobbying, tax transparency)
    • Risk-to-firm vs harm-to-society orientation
    They are not rating the same object.
    2) Measurement differences — how to measure it? most important
    Even when judging the same issue (e.g., labour):
    • Agency A → employee turnover
    • Agency B → lawsuits
    • Agency C → policies
    Explains the majority of divergence (56%, Berg, Koelbel & Rigobon [2022]).
    3) Weighting differences — what matters more?
    • Climate vs governance vs human capital priorities
    • Different values → different final scores
    More disclosure solves information problems.
    It does not solve value-judgment problems.
    Question: Should tax avoidance affect an ESG rating?
    ESG disclosure increases transparency — but sustainability evaluation still requires judgment.
