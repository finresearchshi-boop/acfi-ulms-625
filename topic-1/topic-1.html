    UK political risks of adopting net zero by 2050

    risk channels
    1) Cost-of-living backlash
    Net zero framed as higher bills, fuel costs, or retrofit costs → pressure to dilute policy.
    2) Distributional conflict
    Uneven impacts (motorists, farmers, heavy industry, oil & gas regions, freight/logistics) → lobbying, exemptions, stop–start policy.
    3) Policy reversal risk
    Targets may persist while instruments change: ICE phase-out, heat policy, building standards, planning rules for renewables/grid, carbon pricing design.
    4) Credibility & delivery risk
    Grid build-out, permitting, skills, public investment. If delivery lags, later “catch-up” tightening can create cliff-edge transition risk.
    5) Culture-war / anti-ESG politics
    Reputational double bind: too slow → activist/regulatory pressure; too visible → backlash and consumer pushback.
    6) IAS exclusion flashpoint
    Excluding aviation/shipping can be contested (“not the full picture”) vs resistance to tougher measures on flying/shipping.

    The Turing Pharmaceuticals Case

    Context
    • Turing Pharmaceuticals acquired U.S. rights to Daraprim (pyrimethamine)
    • Decades-old, off-patent drug used to treat toxoplasmosis
    • Critical for immunocompromised patients
    Key actions after acquisition (2015)
    • Price increased from $13.50 per pill to $750 per pill (>5,000% increase overnight)
    • Distribution tightly controlled through limited channels
    • Company argued higher prices would fund R&D and future innovation
    Immediate outcome
    • Revenues per unit rose dramatically
    • Access and affordability concerns emerged almost immediately

    The Shareholder Logic

    From a shareholder-value perspective
    • ✔ Legal acquisition of an undervalued asset
    • ✔ Life-saving drug → highly inelastic demand
    • ✔ Limited short-run competition → monopoly power
    • ✔ Large price increase maximises short-term profits
    Implicit assumptions
    • Firm’s primary duty is to maximise shareholder value
    • Market forces or future entry will correct excess pricing
    • As long as actions are legal, ethical concerns are secondary
    From a narrow shareholder perspective, this looks like rational value maximisation.

    Stakeholders, Backlash, and the Limits of Shareholder Primacy

    Stakeholders affected
    • Patients: reduced affordability and access
    • Hospitals & insurers: sharp cost increases, rationing
    • Doctors: constrained treatment options
    • Society & regulators: erosion of trust
    Consequences
    • Intense public and political backlash
    • U.S. congressional scrutiny of drug pricing
    • Renewed calls for:
      • Faster generic entry
      • Pricing oversight for essential medicines
      • Greater corporate accountability
    The case illustrates the limits of shareholder primacy when social costs are large and visible.

    Mini Case Study
    Corporate purpose in action
    Pharma Global health Purpose

    Merck & Co., Mectizan, and the Meaning of Corporate Purpose

    Background (late 1970s)

    Merck was a leading global pharmaceutical firm with heavy R&D investment and a culture shaped by George W. Merck’s philosophy:

    “Medicine is for the people. It is not for the profits.”

    At the same time, river blindness (onchocerciasis) harmed tens of millions across parts of Africa, Latin America, and the Middle East—causing severe itching, skin damage, and blindness.

    The scientific opportunity

    Merck researchers (led by Dr. William C. Campbell) found ivermectin—originally developed for veterinary use— was effective against the parasite causing river blindness.

    The constraints
    • Human formulation would cost millions
    • Affected populations were extremely poor
    • No viable commercial market
    • No obvious funding/distribution mechanism at scale
    The dilemma
    Should Merck invest in and distribute a life-saving drug that would almost certainly never pay for itself?
    Decision & Impact
    Stakeholder vs shareholder
    1987 Mectizan Enterprise value

    The Decision (1987) and What It Created

    The commitment

    CEO Roy Vagelos committed that Merck would donate Mectizan (ivermectin):

    “As much as needed, for as long as needed, to anyone who needs it.”
    Long-run public health impact
    • Longest-running disease-specific drug donation program
    • Billions of treatments delivered; hundreds of millions reached annually
    • Contributed to elimination certification in Colombia, Ecuador, Guatemala, Mexico
    • River blindness no longer a major public-health issue in parts of West Africa
    Enterprise outcomes
    • Reputation surged; Merck became a benchmark for purpose-led leadership
    • Talent attraction: employees reported joining to solve major human problems
    • Merck remained a highly admired firm over time
    Investor relevance

    Today, thanks in part to this reputation, Merck is among the world’s largest pharmaceutical firms, worth over $200bn. It remains a sought-after employer and is still on Fortune’s “most admired” list.

    Investors have benefited too: since 1978, shareholders reportedly earned around 13% average annual returns—about 1.5× the 9% delivered by the S&P 500.

    Discussion question
    How should firms evaluate investments whose returns are indirect and realised through reputation, trust, and human capital rather than immediate cash flow?

    Layoffs Done Right vs. Done Wrong

    Stakeholders in practice
    Commercial reality: sometimes layoffs are necessary for long-term survival — the difference is how firms treat affected stakeholders.
    Case 1 Airbnb (2020) Humane restructuring
    • Context: COVID travel collapse; ~25% workforce reduction
    • Actions: ≥14 weeks severance; 12 months health insurance; kept laptops; outplacement support
    • Outcome: CEO letter praised; trust preserved among staff, alumni, future hires
    Case 2 Nokia (2008 → 2011) Learning the hard way
    Bochum closure (2008)
    • 2,300 jobs lost; minimal support
    • 15,000 protest; political backlash; boycott calls
    • Viral “crushed phones”; estimated €700m lost sales, €100m lost profits (2008–2010)
    Bridge Programme (2011)
    • 18,000 layoffs across 13 countries
    • €50m Bridge programme (five paths: redeploy, outplace, entrepreneurship, retrain, new paths)
    • 60% knew next step on exit day; no protests; employer brand stabilised
    Key takeaway: layoffs may be economically necessary — but how workers are treated determines whether value is created or destroyed. Employees aren’t just costs; they’re the firm’s most powerful ambassadors.

    Value Creation vs CSR: Growing the Pie vs Sharing It

    CSR (necessary, but not sufficient)
    What CSR typically does
    • Mitigates harm or reputational risk
    • Redistributes a small part of profits
    • Often peripheral to the core business
    Examples
    • Donations by a tobacco company
    • Philanthropy while core products cause addiction
    • “Green” initiatives while core operations pollute
    Key limitation
    CSR does not change how profits are made — only how they are spent.
    Value creation (serving society through the core business)
    What value creation does
    • Improves welfare through the core business
    • Uses the firm’s expertise and comparative advantage
    • Profits arise because society benefits
    Examples
    • Airbnb offerd generous severance, extended healthcare, and active outplacement support
    • Merck developing and donating Mectizan
    • Innovation that improves health, safety, or efficiency
    Key principle
    Profits are earned only by creating value for society.
    The central distinction
    It doesn’t matter how many charitable acts a firm undertakes if its core business model creates social harm. The goal is not redistribution as “stakeholder capitalism”, but profit through value creation for society.
    Growing the pie ≠ de-growth. Growth can mean better health, skills, efficiency, trust, and sustainability — not just higher revenues.
    EXAMPLES
    Value creation through the core business
    Redeploy Repurpose Reallocate

    Additional examples of value creation (COVID)

    Redeploy capabilities
    • Chelsea FC — free hotel accommodation for frontline NHS staff (COVID-19).
    • JetBlue — grounded aircraft used to move medical staff and supplies.
    • Qantas — furloughed staff redeployed into grocery roles via Woolworths.
    • English National Opera — breathing expertise applied to long-COVID recovery.
    • Barry’s — boutique fitness studio offered free online workouts and wellbeing content during lockdowns.
    Repurpose production & resources
    • LVMH — perfume lines repurposed to produce and donate hand sanitiser.
    • Mercedes + UCL/UCLH — engineered CPAP breathing aids; designs shared globally for free.

    • Burberry — shifted parts of its supply chain to produce PPE (including gowns and masks) for health workers.
    Pattern: the firm’s comparative advantage is redirected to meet urgent social needs — value is created through the core business.

    Friedman (1970) • Shareholder value • CSR nuance

    The “profits only” view is a caricature — it’s conditional

    Maximise profits as a benchmark; deviate only when assumptions fail: comparative advantage, accountability, feasibility.

    Why profits can serve society

    Pluralism via dividends

    • Investors value different causes: A → racial equality B → environment
    • Higher profits → higher dividends → individuals fund preferred causes (or education, health, etc.)
    • Prevents CEOs selecting “worthy” causes on everyone’s behalf
    Benchmark: decentralize moral choice when firms lack special advantage in “doing good”.
    HOW TO READ FRIEDMAN
    Profit maximisation is the default benchmark. Deviate only when firms can create more impact per £1 than investors or governments can.
    New York Times Magazine (1970) — a decision rule framed by assumptions.
    When profit maximisation fails

    Comparative advantage matters

    • If the firm can create more impact per £1, it should act e.g., reduce plastic packaging
    • Operational levers beat cheques: hiring, promotion, culture, product design
    • Good CSR = actions only the firm can control well
    Example: Instead of symbolic donations, invest in representation, evaluation fairness, and anti-discrimination systems.
    Why regulation alone is insufficient
    🏛️ Lobbying power can distort regulation
    🧩 Hard to regulate qualitative issues (culture, dignity, skills)
    ⏳ Slow political cycles; firms face timely investor scrutiny
    Takeaway: corporate action is strongest when it leverages firm-specific levers and stays accountable.
    Friedman “profit-max” benchmark • Exception: firm comparative advantage

    When firms have comparative advantage, “profits-only” becomes a bad decision rule

    In some contexts, firms can deliver faster, larger, or cheaper solutions than individuals or governments — because they control design, R&D, infrastructure, and scaling.

    Case 1
    Apple — packaging redesign
    Comparative advantage Product + supply-chain design
    • Cut plastic at massive scale by changing packaging engineering & supplier standards.
    • Commitment / progress: eliminate plastic in packaging (target 2025); packaging ~98%+ fibre-based; some product packaging became 100% fibre-based (limited exceptions).
    Why “profits-only” fails
    Core design choices can deliver more impact per £ than individuals can through donations/consumption choices alone.
    Case 2
    Pfizer–BioNTech — COVID-19 vaccine
    Comparative advantage R&D + trials + manufacturing + distribution
    • Private R&D and manufacturing networks compress science → supply timelines.
    • Illustration: UK authorisation on 2 Dec 2020 highlights how fast execution can scale life-saving innovation.
    Why “profits-only” fails
    In pandemics, the social value of speed and scale is enormous; governments can fund/regulate, but firms execute end-to-end.
    Case 3
    Starlink in Ukraine — emergency connectivity
    Comparative advantage Satellite infrastructure + rapid deployment
    • Rapid delivery of terminals helped restore comms when terrestrial networks were degraded.
    • Context: after the 2022 invasion, thousands were rushed in to enable resilience.
    Why “profits-only” fails
    Individuals and governments can’t quickly build substitute infrastructure; a firm with the asset can provide immediate resilience.
    Case 4
    AstraZeneca & J&J — not-for-profit COVID supply
    Comparative advantage Manufacturing scale + global access
    • Committed to supply vaccines on a not-for-profit basis during the pandemic.
    • Signal: in extreme public-health emergencies, firms may suspend pure profit-max to scale access.
    Why “profits-only” fails
    When marginal social benefits are extraordinary, “profit-max” can underweight access and urgency.
    Takeaway: Treat profit maximisation as a benchmark — but override it when a firm’s unique capabilities (design/R&D/infrastructure/scale) create more social value per £ than individuals or governments can.
    Creating Value vs. Extracting Value • Two food industry cases

    Profits can be earned by solving problems — or taken by shifting costs to society

    Compare a case where incentives were redesigned to cut emissions and costs, versus a case where short-term sales relied on externalising health risks.

    CASE 1 • Value creation
    Walkers Crisps
    Trigger Environmental concern → life-cycle carbon audit (Carbon Trust)
    Insight Major emissions hotspot: potato drying
    Root cause Buying by gross weight incentivised farmers to add water
    Action Switched to dry-weight purchasing (fix the incentive)
    Outcomes (≈ 2 years)
    ↓ 7%
    carbon per packet
    ↓ 4,800
    tonnes CO₂
    £400k
    energy saved / yr
    Logic
    Fixing incentives reduced emissions and costs → environmental value creation aligned with shareholder value.
    CASE 2 • Value extraction
    Nestlé Infant Formula
    Strategy (1970s) Aggressive marketing in developing countries; reps dressed as nurses; free samples
    Mechanism Mothers stopped lactating → locked into formula purchases
    Risk shifted Unsafe prep (clean water, sanitation, literacy); over-dilution → malnutrition
    Legality Not illegal at the time — but socially harmful
    Backlash & value destruction
    Global boycott (from 1977)
    ≈ $1bn lost sales (by 1984)
    Long-lasting reputational damage (still cited)
    Logic
    Profits extracted by externalising health risks → social harm ultimately destroyed shareholder value.
    Bottom line: Profits earned by solving social problems tend to endure; profits earned by exploiting them rarely do.
    Illustrative teaching cases: Walkers/Carbon Trust life-cycle audit (2007+) and Nestlé infant formula controversy & boycott history.
    Transition • Purpose → Materiality & disclosure incentives

    From Purpose to Proof: decisions you can point to

    Purpose isn’t a slogan — it should show up in incentives, trade-offs, and measurable outcomes.

    What we’ve learned
    Purpose → direction
    Purpose = why a firm exists and who it serves
    Profits are a by-product of solving real problems
    Purpose helps navigate trade-offs and resource allocation
    “Profits are to business as breathing is to life. Breathing is essential to life, but is not the purpose for living. Similarly, profits are essential for the existence of the corporation, but they are not the reason for its existence.” — Dennis Bakke (Founder of energy supplier AES)
    Evidence
    Purpose showing up in decisions
    Three cases (signals you can audit)
    Unilever handwashing mission → 1bn people reached (target hit 2 yrs early); “Sustainable Living Brands” grew 69% faster (2018)
    CVS stopped selling cigarettes (gave up ~$2bn sales) shortly before renaming itself CVS Health → sales rose $139bn (2014) → $185bn (2017)
    Barclays closed tax-avoidance activity (gave up ~£1bn revenue) → exited “legal but misaligned” business to protect legitimacy & trust
    Bridge: These are claims with observable footprints — the next step is learning what must be disclosed so outsiders can judge credibility.
