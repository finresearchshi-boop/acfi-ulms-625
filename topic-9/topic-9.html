    Topic 9
    ESG Data Providers and Capital Markets

    Why ESG data providers matter

    Most investors do not read every sustainability report from first page to last. Intermediaries collect, standardize, estimate, weight, and transform corporate disclosure into usable signals.
    What data providers do
    Collect
    Pull information from reports, filings, websites, news, NGO sources, and direct company responses.
    Standardize
    Translate heterogeneous disclosure into common variables.
    Estimate
    Fill gaps where firms do not disclose cleanly or consistently.
    Aggregate
    Convert many indicators into scores, ratings, rankings, or index eligibility.
    Why this is economically important
    In practice, many market participants do not trade on raw sustainability data. They trade on intermediated interpretations of that data.
    • ESG providers influence comparability.
    • They shape what becomes visible.
    • They can affect capital allocation, stewardship, screening, and product design.
    Question: If the intermediary is influential, should firms disclose for stakeholders, for investors, or for raters?
    Bridge from Topic 8
    Topic 8 focused on whether disclosure is credible; Topic 9 asks what happens after the disclosure leaves the firm.

    Major ESG data providers and how they differ

    These names are often grouped together, but they do not all produce the same kind of ESG output.
    Ratings and risk systems
    MSCI
    AAA-to-CCC style company and fund ratings focused on resilience to industry-specific sustainability risks.
    Sustainalytics
    ESG Risk Ratings focused on exposure to material ESG issues and how well firms manage that risk.
    S&P Global
    Corporate Sustainability Assessment, ESG scores, raw data, and sustainability index construction.
    ISS ESG
    Strong on governance, stewardship, proxy-voting integration, and climate and controversy tools.
    Moody's
    Links ESG and climate analytics to physical risk, transition risk, and credit-risk frameworks.
    Data, disclosure, and index specialists
    LSEG
    Transparent, public-data-based ESG scores with modular separation between performance, risk, and impact.
    Bloomberg
    Terminal-based sustainability scores and datasets built mainly from public company disclosures.
    CDP
    Specialized environmental disclosure and letter scores on climate, water, and forests rather than one all-in-one ESG rating.
    FTSE Russell
    ESG scores and index tools that emphasize exposure-weighted materiality and benchmark use.
    RepRisk
    Outside-in business conduct and controversy signals from media, NGO, and stakeholder sources.
    Key distinction
    Some providers emphasize ratings, some unmanaged ESG risk, some disclosure and climate data, and some outside-in controversy signals.

    Why ESG ratings disagree: scope, measurement, weight

    Ratings can diverge without any provider being careless. Differences often come from what counts, how it is measured, and how much weight each issue receives.
    Scope disagreement
    Scope
    Providers include different indicators, controversies, geographies, and business-segment exposures.
    Effect
    Two raters may evaluate the same firm but not the same object. For example, one may assess the whole group, while another gives more weight to a controversial subsidiary or business segment.
    Measurement and weighting disagreement
    Measure
    The same issue may be operationalized using different indicators, such as policy disclosure, observed outcome, estimated value, or controversy-based assessment. For example, carbon performance may be measured using a net-zero commitment, reported emissions intensity, or model-estimated emissions.
    Weight
    Providers assign different importance to E, S, and G components and to sector-specific issues.
    Disagreement therefore does not necessarily imply error. It may arise because providers construct different indicators from the same underlying information and then apply different weights.
    Anchor concept
    Treat ESG disagreement as structural, not just random error.

    From corporate disclosure to market signal

    It is easy to treat the firm’s report, the provider’s score, and the investor’s decision as the same object. They are not.
    Firm disclosure
    Metrics, targets, narrative, controversies, restatements, boundaries.
    →
    Provider methodology
    Scope choice, estimation, weighting, category logic, controversy treatment.
    →
    Investor interpretation
    Screening, valuation, stewardship, risk control, marketing, fund construction.
    →
    Capital-market outcome
    Flows, pricing, engagement priorities, cost of capital, index inclusion.
    Key takeaway
    Disagreement can enter at every stage: firms disclose selectively, providers interpret differently, and investors use the outputs for different purposes.
    Analytical rule
    Do not ask only “what did the company disclose?” Ask also “how was it translated, by whom, and for what use?”

    Investors do not all use ESG information in the same way

    Different investors use ESG information for different purposes, so the same signal can support different decisions.
    Different uses
    Screening
    Exclude firms or sectors that fail a minimum standard or violate mandate constraints.
    Best-in-class
    Select relative leaders within a sector rather than excluding whole sectors.
    Risk
    Use ESG information as one input into downside risk, controversy risk, or transition risk analysis.
    Stewardship
    Use disclosure and ratings to prioritize engagement and voting rather than immediate trading.
    Key reminder
    A low ESG score does not automatically mean “sell”, and a high score does not automatically mean “buy”.
    • Some investors care about financial materiality.
    • Some care about values alignment.
    • Some care about engagement leverage.
    • Some care mainly about product labeling and mandate compliance.
    Important distinction
    The same ESG signal can support very different decisions depending on investor objective.

    Why divergence matters for capital markets

    If providers disagree, firms may face a noisier information environment, and investors may price or react to sustainability information less consistently.

    Provider D
    One firm can look very different across providers.
    Likely consequences
    • More uncertainty for investors trying to compare firms.
    • Stronger incentives to manage disclosure so firms reduce coding gaps and improve visibility.
    • Slower or less consistent pricing of sustainability information in asset markets.
    • More influence for intermediaries in shaping what “good ESG performance” means.
    Question: Does disagreement reduce the usefulness of ESG information, or does it show that sustainability is multi-dimensional?
    Deep point
    Divergence can itself be information: it signals ambiguity, contested materiality, or model dependence.

    How ratings disagreement shapes disclosure strategy

    Once firms realize that intermediaries affect market perception, they may start disclosing not only for stakeholders, but also for coders, analysts, and index methodologies.
    What firms are tempted to do
    Improve visibility
    Make data easier for providers to find, classify, and compare.
    Target metrics
    Prioritize indicators believed to affect influential ratings or products.
    Reduce gaps
    Fill obvious disclosure holes that trigger estimation penalties.
    Manage to score
    Optimize toward methodology rather than toward underlying sustainability performance.
    What to watch for
    Once a measure becomes a target, it can be managed strategically. The same logic applies to market intermediaries.
    • Better disclosure can genuinely reduce information asymmetry.
    • But disclosure can also become score-oriented performance management.
    • This links directly back to greenwashing and assurance from Topic 8.

    Case study — Tesla and ESG ratings disagreement

    The public evidence does not all point in one direction: the same company was included, removed, and then re-added in S&P’s ESG index over a short period.
    What happened
    2021
    Tesla entered the S&P 500 ESG Index on May 1, 2021. Although S&P ranked Tesla fifth out of five in the S&P 500 auto group, adding it brought the industry group closer to S&P’s target of about 75% of float-adjusted market capitalization.
    2022
    Tesla was removed effective May 2, 2022. S&P said Tesla fell into the bottom 25% of its global GICS industry-group peers and pointed to weaker criteria on low-carbon strategy and codes of business conduct, plus controversy exposure linked to discrimination claims and the U.S. National Highway Traffic Safety Administration (NHTSA) Autopilot investigation.
    2023
    Tesla returned in the 2023 rebalance. Reuters reported that S&P said Tesla added disclosure on physical climate risk and on ESG considerations in its supply-chain management strategy.
    What this case shows
    • Tesla’s product story is strong: Tesla’s 2024 impact report says customers avoided nearly 32 million metric tons of CO2e.
    • But ESG judgments also depend on labor, safety, governance, controversy, disclosure, and peer-relative methodology.
    • Reuters, CNBC, Bloomberg, and Fortune all highlighted the “Tesla out, Exxon in” paradox. Their common point was that this S&P index measures peer-relative ESG risk and disclosure quality, not simply climate impact.
    • Berg, Kölbel, and Rigobon (2022) find that divergence comes mainly from measurement (56%), then scope (38%), and much less from weight (6%).
    Question: If one provider emphasizes Tesla’s product impact while another emphasizes controversy, governance, and disclosure, should we expect them to reach the same ESG judgment? Probably not, because they are effectively judging different things.
    Main point
    This example shows that ESG disagreement is not just theoretical: different methodologies can produce very different judgments about the same company.

    Why fund labels and names matter

    Once ESG enters financial products, sustainability language becomes a regulatory issue. This is where ratings, disclosure, and anti-greenwashing policy meet.
    Key policy developments
    UK
    Anti-greenwashing and sustainability-label rules push firms toward claims that are fair, clear, and evidence-based.
    EU
    ESMA fund-name rules and related sustainable-finance guidance aim to reduce exaggerated sustainability marketing.
    Key takeaway
    The market does not only regulate corporate reports. It increasingly regulates the language used to sell sustainability-related financial products.
    • Scores affect product design.
    • Labels affect investor expectations.
    • Rules aim to narrow the gap between name, process, and portfolio reality.
    Applied angle
    This shows why ESG data providers matter beyond academic debate: they shape what can be marketed credibly in capital markets.

    Why different actors read the same ESG information differently

    Use the same company pack, but approach it with a different objective.
    Group 1: ESG rating provider
    Choose variables, justify weights, and explain what your score is actually measuring.
    Group 2: Long-only investor
    Decide whether the information changes valuation, risk perception, or stewardship priority.
    Group 3: Company IR / sustainability team
    Explain how you would improve disclosure to reduce misunderstanding and improve comparability.
    Group 4: Regulator / consumer advocate
    Identify where product naming, score presentation, or selective disclosure could mislead end users.
    What you should notice
    Disagreement is not necessarily a flaw. It can follow from different roles and different definitions of what ESG information is for.

    Takeaways and sources

    The core logic is simple: sustainability information does not move directly from firm to market. It moves through intermediaries, methodologies, and investor objectives.
    Key takeaways
    • ESG data providers translate messy disclosure into investable signals.
    • Ratings divergence often reflects differences in scope, measurement, and weighting.
    • Investors use ESG information for different purposes, so the same signal can support different decisions.
    • Disclosure strategy increasingly responds to intermediary methodologies and anti-greenwashing rules.
