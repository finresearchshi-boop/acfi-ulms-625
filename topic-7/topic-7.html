    Topic 7
    Measuring Social and Governance Performance

    Why social and governance metrics are harder than carbon metrics

    Environmental metrics often start with physical quantities. Social and governance metrics often start with behaviour, institutions, culture, and incentives.
    Environmental performance
    • Often rooted in physical units such as energy, water, or CO₂e.
    • Measurement can be imperfect, but the underlying object is still material and observable.
    • Students can usually ask: what is the quantity?
    vs
    Social & governance performance
    • Usually measured using proxies: turnover, complaints, independence ratios, training hours.
    • The underlying construct may be trust, fairness, culture, oversight, or incentive quality.
    • The harder question becomes: what exactly is this number standing in for?
    A company can score well on policy disclosure and still perform badly in practice.
    Core distinction
    In social and governance, many numbers are not direct measures; they are interpretive signals.

    What counts as social performance?

    “Social” usually covers the firm’s relationships with workers, suppliers, customers, and communities — but each of those can be measured at the level of policy, process, or outcome.
    A useful measurement ladder
    Policy
    Codes of conduct, diversity policies, human-rights statements, grievance channels.
    Process
    Training hours, audits completed, supplier checks, helpline usage, committee meetings.
    Outcome
    Injury rates, wage violations, harassment claims, employee retention, customer harm.
    How to read these metrics
    • Policy metrics are easier to disclose.
    • Process metrics are easier to monitor.
    • Outcome metrics are often what outsiders actually care about.
    Discussion question: If a company reports 100% supplier-code coverage, does that necessarily mean workers are treated fairly in practice?
    Interpretation rule
    The closer the metric is to outcomes, the more informative it may be — but usually the harder it is to produce cleanly.

    Social metrics across the firm boundary

    Unlike carbon, many social harms happen outside the legal entity: in subcontractors, logistics partners, gig work, or digital ecosystems.
    Where the measurement problem appears
    • Own employees vs agency workers
    • Tier-1 suppliers vs subcontractors
    • Customers vs platform users
    • Formal controls vs informal culture
    A practical audit chain
    1
    Define the population: who is exposed to the risk?
    2
    Define the boundary: does the firm control, influence, or merely transact?
    3
    Choose the evidence: self-report, audit, complaints, regulator findings, media, litigation?
    4
    Interpret silence: is no complaint equal to no problem?
    Boundary problem
    A recurring question for Topic 7: where does the firm’s responsibility end?

    Governance indicators and incentives

    Governance metrics often measure structure — board composition, committee independence, compensation design — but the deeper issue is whether those structures actually discipline behaviour.
    Common governance indicators
    Oversight
    Board independence, separation of CEO/chair, risk committee existence.
    Incentives
    Bonus plans, clawbacks, pay-performance sensitivity, ESG-linked pay.
    Controls
    Internal audit, whistleblowing channels, compliance functions, escalation routes.
    Critical lens
    A board can look independent on paper while still failing to challenge strategy, culture, or incentives.
    • Structure is visible and easy to disclose.
    • Behaviour is harder to observe and much more consequential.
    • Incentive design often reveals more than glossy governance statements.
    Discussion question: Which governance signal would you trust more — 90% independent directors, or a compensation system with a credible clawback and fewer aggressive sales targets?
    Key distinction
    Governance quality is not the same as governance architecture.

    Case study — Wells Fargo and the fake-accounts scandal

    This case shows how a company can have many performance metrics and still fail badly if those metrics reward the wrong behaviour.
    What happened: For years, branch staff faced intense pressure to meet cross-selling targets. Employees responded by opening millions of unauthorized deposit and credit-card accounts, moving customer funds, and creating products customers had not genuinely requested.
    What was monitored
    • Cross-selling targets
    • Sales volumes
    • Daily branch performance
    What employees faced
    • Pressure to hit unrealistic goals
    • Fear of discipline for missing quotas
    • A culture that normalised gaming
    What followed
    • Customer harm and reputational damage
    • Regulatory penalties and public scrutiny
    • Questions about board oversight and incentive design
    Main lesson
    “What gets monitored gets done” can be more accurate than “what gets measured gets managed.” Employees often manage the monitor.
    Discussion question: If branch managers are judged mainly by sales count, what behaviour may become privately rational even when it harms customers?
    Incentive distortion
    Wells Fargo is a powerful example of Goodhart’s Law: once a metric becomes a target, behaviour can become distorted.

    Case study — Uber, workplace culture, and governance failure

    Uber is useful because it shows how outstanding business performance can coexist with serious internal social and governance failures.
    What happened
    • Uber grew extremely quickly, expanding across markets and attracting major investor attention.
    • At the same time, former employees raised allegations involving harassment, retaliation, weak HR responses, and poor accountability.
    • The scandal raised broader questions about leadership behaviour, internal controls, and whether complaints were taken seriously.
    A simple scorecard lens
    Visible to investors
    growth, rides, market share, fundraising, expansion.
    Harder to see
    psychological safety, complaint handling, managerial accountability, retaliation risk.
    Discussion question: If culture is one of a firm’s most important intangible assets, why do companies disclose so few credible culture metrics?
    Culture as data problem
    Uber helps students see that revenue growth and user growth do not automatically mean strong social performance.

    How firms choose ESG KPIs

    Companies rarely measure everything. They choose indicators that are available, auditable, benchmarkable, and defensible — which means difficult issues can disappear from the dashboard.
    What matters socially
    Worker dignity, discrimination, customer harm, governance challenge, safety culture.
    →
    What can be counted
    Training hours, policies, turnover, independent directors, committee meetings.
    →
    What gets disclosed
    A small set of indicators that look comparable and manageable.
    Critical insight
    What gets measured is often not what matters most. It is what is easiest to standardise.
    Discussion question: Which important social harms are often missing from standard ESG dashboards?
    KPI bias
    A dashboard is always a theory of what matters.

    Case study — Amazon, productivity monitoring, and worker outcomes

    Amazon is useful because it combines sophisticated measurement systems with debate over whether close monitoring improves efficiency at the cost of worker wellbeing and safety.
    What happened
    • Warehouse operations increasingly relied on detailed metrics such as scan rates, task times, downtime, and fulfilment speed.
    • These systems helped Amazon manage scale and delivery performance very effectively.
    • But critics argued that the same systems could intensify pressure, reduce worker discretion, and contribute to injuries, stress, or high turnover.
    Discussion question
    If a firm reports superb delivery performance, should investors also ask for injury rates, turnover, worker grievances, and ergonomic risk data?
    What this case teaches
    Measurement can be both a tool of optimisation and a source of social risk.
    • Managerial view: more data improves control.
    • Social view: more monitoring can intensify pressure and shift hidden costs onto workers.
    Trade-off slide
    This case is useful because the same metric system can be interpreted as both operational excellence and a source of worker harm.

    Case study — Boeing 737 MAX and the limits of formal governance metrics

    Boeing illustrates how board structure, compliance language, and formal processes may still fail to capture deeper cultural and strategic pressures.
    What happened
    Competition pressure
    Boeing faced pressure to respond quickly to market competition in narrow-body aircraft.
    Engineering concerns
    Technical design and certification issues became major safety concerns rather than routine engineering matters.
    Crashes and aftermath
    Two fatal crashes led to global grounding of the 737 MAX and intense scrutiny of safety culture, oversight, and decision-making.
    Why it belongs in Topic 7
    • It shows that governance failure can look like culture failure.
    • Typical governance indicators — independence, committees, policies — may not reveal whether dissent is genuinely heard.
    • The firm’s most important missing metric may have been the quality of challenge inside decision-making.
    Discussion question: What metric, if any, might have warned outsiders that safety culture was deteriorating?
    Culture and challenge
    Boeing helps students see why governance is about power, escalation, and challenge, not only structure.

    Case study — Facebook / Meta, data governance, and social harm

    This case broadens Topic 7 beyond labour. It asks how we measure customer welfare, privacy, platform governance, and downstream social consequences.
    What happened: Facebook built a hugely successful advertising platform based on user engagement and data. But repeated controversies raised concerns about privacy, third-party data access, content governance, and whether the platform’s incentives amplified wider social harms.
    What looked successful
    • User growth
    • Engagement
    • Ad monetisation
    • Product scaling
    What the case raises
    • Privacy governance
    • Data access and controls
    • Content moderation incentives
    • Who bears social externalities?
    Customer metric
    Satisfaction may miss data misuse risk.
    Governance metric
    Committee existence may miss platform design incentives.
    Social metric
    The hardest harms may emerge outside the firm boundary.
    Platform governance
    Useful for showing that social performance includes customers and society, not only employees.

    What do ESG ratings actually measure?

    One of the most important empirical lessons is that different ESG providers often disagree substantially. That is not just noise; it tells us ESG is partly a problem of definition, scope, and weighting.

    How to interpret this slide
    • Some ratings are closer to financial risk to the firm.
    • Others try to capture the firm’s impact on stakeholders.
    • Different methodologies create different scores, even for the same company.
    Divergence is not necessarily a flaw in data collection alone; it is also a disagreement about what ESG is supposed to mean.
