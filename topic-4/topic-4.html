    Topic 4
    Structure and content of sustainability reports

    The Three Structural Tensions of Sustainability Reporting

    The report is not “one thing”. It is an uneasy settlement between rules, judgement, and persuasion.
    1) Standardisation vs Managerial Discretion
    Standardization
    GRI / ESRS / SASB → comparability, minimum disclosure architecture, auditability.
    Discretion
    Materiality thresholds, boundary choices, estimation methods, narrative framing.
    Failure mode: “Compliance theatre” — high format compliance, low decision-useful content.
    2) Backward Measurement vs Forward Uncertainty
    Metrics are historical
    Emissions, injuries, training hours: measurable only after the fact.
    Strategy is forward-looking
    Targets, transition plans, resilience: scenario-based, model-dependent, uncertain.
    Failure mode: “Precision bias” — reliable numbers dominate attention; uncertain risks get rhetorical treatment.
    3) Accountability vs Legitimacy Signalling
    Accountability
    Auditable, verifiable, governed; ideally linked to incentives and controls.
    Legitimacy signalling
    A report also “markets” seriousness to regulators, investors, employees, civil society.
    Failure mode: “Selective assurance” — credibility is borrowed from assured fragments.
    Provocation: “Most sustainability reports are compliance-driven architectures constrained by uncertainty.” These tensions are not accidental — they arise from economic and institutional constraints.

    Why These Tensions Are Structural

    These tensions arise from economic limits of disclosure, deep uncertainty, and institutional pressures — not from poor report design.

    Incomplete Verifiability (Information Economics)

    Milgrom (1981): full disclosure requires credible verification. Sustainability disclosures are only partially verifiable — materiality thresholds, boundaries, and estimates remain discretionary. Standards force partial unravelling, but discretion survives.

    Knightian Uncertainty (Risk Theory)

    Knight (1921): risk is measurable; uncertainty is not. Climate transition risk is largely non-probabilistic. Accounting systems privilege observable, historical numbers, structurally disadvantaging forward uncertainty.

    Performativity of Quantification

    Porter (1995); Espeland & Stevens (1998): numbers do not merely describe reality — they construct it. What is measurable becomes governable. Reliable historical metrics dominate attention over uncertain strategic risks.

    Institutional Legitimacy Pressures

    Meyer & Rowan (1977); Suchman (1995): organizations adopt formal structures to secure legitimacy. Sustainability reporting may exhibit decoupling — compliance architecture without full operational transformation.

    Milgrom (1981) · Knight (1921) · Porter (1995) · Espeland & Stevens (1998) · Meyer & Rowan (1977) · Suchman (1995)
    Emphasise: These tensions are structural constraints, not managerial incompetence. Move from structure → theory → then Sage case illustration.

    Sustainability Report as a Hybrid Document

    Treat the report as four “zones”. Each zone solves a different problem — and creates a different risk.
    Zone
    Function
    Risk
    Governance
    Legitimacy & accountability: who decides, who monitors, who signs off.
    Box-ticking: committees exist, but decision rights and escalation are vague.
    Metrics
    Comparability: stable indicators across firms, time, and standards.
    Narrow measurement: what’s measurable crowds out what matters.
    Narrative
    Strategic positioning: interpret performance, justify trade-offs, frame uncertainty.
    Greenwashing: asymmetric disclosure + cherry-picked stories.
    Targets
    Forward commitment: define trajectory, milestones, and implied capital allocation.
    Unrealistic optimism: targets without credible pathways or governance “teeth”.
    Analytical move
    Evaluate a report by asking: Which zone is doing the heavy lifting?
    (Often: “assured metrics” carry credibility for “unassured narrative”.)
    Discussion question
    Which zone would a regulator care about most? Which zone does marketing care about most?
    This is the slide that makes the whole lecture “structural”. Students can later map Sage features onto zones and see where assurance and governance are concentrated.

    The Measurement Problem

    Financial reporting measures what happened. Sustainability reporting increasingly claims to measure what might happen.
    Financial reporting (baseline)
    • Past-oriented: realised transactions and outcomes.
    • Quantified: accounting rules → stable measurement conventions.
    • Audited: ex-post verification is feasible (evidence exists).
    • Decision logic: performance → valuation, stewardship, contracting.
    Evidence densityhigh
    Counterfactual dependencelow
    Model reliancelow
    Sustainability reporting (frontier)
    • Future risk: climate/transition, litigation, supply chain, human capital.
    • Scenario-based: “what if” pathways — not forecasts.
    • Estimation-heavy: boundaries, scopes, factors, assumptions.
    • Audit gap: forward claims often become “assurance-lite”.
    Proxy metricseasy, incomplete
    Forward-looking metricsplans, still non-financial
    Scenario analysismodelled outcomes
    Rule of thumb: the further forward you look, the weaker “measurement” becomes. So credibility shifts from numbers → governance → assumptions → assurance scope.
    Anchor: BoE Box A distinguishes proxies / forward-looking / scenario analysis and says scenarios are not forecasts. Use that to legitimise why “measurement” is structurally hard, not a managerial failure.

    Reporting Horizon Problem

    A Net-Zero 2040 claim is made today — but what is credible today, and what is merely aspirational?
    A long-term target is cheap to state, expensive to govern.
    Credibility questions (not “PR questions”)
    • What is enforceable? (board oversight, escalation routes, sanctions)
    • What is measurable now? (interim milestones, definitions, boundaries)
    • What is funded? (capex/opex signals, product roadmap, supplier programmes)
    • What is assured? (scope + limitations + materiality threshold)
    Time mismatch
    Financial statements: annual → 3–5 year planning windows
    Sustainability risks: 10–30 years, fat tails, non-linearities
    So: “materiality” becomes a moving target.
    Short horizon: high verifiability, low relevance for transition risk.
    Medium horizon: partial measurability, contested assumptions.
    Long horizon: high relevance, low enforceability without governance “teeth”.
    Key idea: Horizons are a governance choice, not a technical footnote. Next: does the report show real connectivity — or narrative glue?
    Make them articulate: “What would have to be true for the 2040 claim to be credible in 2026?” Then: what evidence would exist in a report that is *not* just rhetoric?

    Connectivity: Real Integration or Narrative Glue?

    Connectivity is the “audit trail” from strategy to metrics. If the chain breaks, the report becomes persuasion without accountability.
    Strategywhat we will do
    Materialitywhat counts
    Riskwhat could go wrong
    Governancewho owns it
    Incentiveswhat gets rewarded
    Targetswhere we aim
    KPIswhat we measure
    Where does the chain typically break?
    Materiality → omission: “immaterial” becomes a managerial discretion lever (topic exclusion).
    Risk → metrics: risks described qualitatively; KPIs report what is easy to count.
    Governance → incentives: committees exist, but pay and decision rights are weakly tied.
    Targets → assurance: targets are ambitious; assurance is partial and backward-looking.
    KPIs → decision-use: numbers do not map into capital allocation or product strategy.
    Diagnostic: “Show me the connective tissue, not the slogans.” Now: Sage as a compact regulatory-architecture case.
    Students can later “score” a report by checking which links have evidence. This sets up the Sage mini-case slides cleanly.

    Sage: Regulatory Architecture in Four “Receipts”

    These four disclosures reveal the scaffolding: materiality gates → time horizons → financial materiality → assurance scope.
    Receipt 1 — Materiality as an inclusion gate
    “We have omitted all the disclosure requirements … as these were deemed immaterial in our DMA.”
    Sage NFS 2025 · “About the Statement”
    What this shows: compliance can be achieved through exclusion (DMA decides “what appears”).
    Receipt 2 — Time horizons are chosen (and justified)
    “Short-term 5 years; medium-term 5–15 years; long-term 15–30 years.”
    Sage NFS 2025 · “Definitions and assumptions”
    What this shows: horizons are a governance choice that shapes what can be “material today”.
    Receipt 3 — Climate: “not financially material”
    “We did not identify that climate is a financially material risk … in the short, medium or long term.”
    Sage NFS 2025 · climate risk & opportunity assessment
    What this shows: financial materiality can diverge from “common sense” salience.
    Receipt 4 — Assurance is explicitly selective
    “Provide limited assurance over selected sustainability disclosure …”
    Sage NFS 2025 · Independent assurance report
    What this shows: credibility is built via assured fragments, not the whole narrative.
    How to read this (method you can reuse on any firm)
    DMA
    Standardisation vs discretion: the firm decides what falls outside the reporting boundary.
    Interpretation move: treat “immaterial” as a decision, not a fact.
    Horizon
    Forward uncertainty: chosen horizons determine which risks are “in scope” for present accountability.
    Interpretation move: ask what becomes invisible when you lengthen/shorten horizons.
    FM
    Financial materiality threshold: “not financially material” depends on an implicit cutoff.
    Interpretation move: ask “what threshold?” and “what evidence?”
    Assure
    Accountability vs legitimacy: assurance produces credibility islands.
    Interpretation move: distinguish “assured KPIs” from “unaudited storyline”.
    One-minute class prompt:
    Which receipt is most consequential for decision-usefulness — omission, horizons, “not financially material”, or selective assurance?

    Sage: Where the Reporting Tensions Become Visible

    We now move from what is disclosed to what the architecture implies.
    1. Omission is rule-consistent
    “Omitted … disclosure requirements … deemed immaterial in our DMA.”
    ESRS structure is followed — but topic inclusion remains discretionary.
    2. Climate: not financially material
    “Did not identify climate as a financially material risk … short, medium or long term.”
    Impact salience ≠ financial materiality.
    3. Assurance is selective
    “Limited assurance over selected sustainability disclosures.”
    Assurance strengthens parts of the report — not the whole narrative.
    4. Incentive weight
    ESG metrics: 5% + 5% in executive incentives.
    Sustainability is embedded — but magnitude matters.
    Three Structural Questions
    A
    Threshold Question
    What scoring threshold converts a topic from “reportable” to “immaterial”? Who sets it? Who reviews it?
    B
    Financial Lens Question
    What magnitude of cash flow effect is required before climate becomes “financially material”?
    C
    Credibility Question
    Does selective assurance increase trust — or create the appearance of completeness?
    In-Class Exercise
    You are a regulator redesigning ESRS.

    What single change would reduce discretion without making reporting infeasible?
    • Mandatory quantitative DMA thresholds?
    • Full-report assurance instead of selected KPIs?
    • Mandatory disclosure of financial materiality cutoff?
