    Topic 5–6
    Measuring Environmental Performance: Climate and Carbon

    Measurement as governance infrastructure

    Environmental performance metrics are not neutral; they allocate responsibility, shape incentives, and create accountability at scale.
    Why measurement exists
    • Capital markets: compare firms, price climate risk, set covenants.
    • Regulators: enforce disclosure + targets.
    • Operations: identify abatement options, track progress.
    • Legitimacy: credibility of commitments; greenwashing constraints.
    A key claim
    “What gets measured gets managed” — but what gets measured also gets strategically managed.
    Measurement creates a ‘shadow price’
    • Once quantified, emissions become a budget line (fees, internal prices, cap‑and‑trade exposure).
    • Metrics turn diffuse impacts into auditable numbers.
    Critical lens
    • Who designs the metric?
    • What is excluded?
    • Where are the incentives to bias?
    → Treat carbon numbers like accounting numbers: useful, but contestable.
    Measurement Shapes Incentives
    Bridge to financial accounting: boundaries, recognition, audit trail.

    Climate risk vs environmental performance

    Two distinct objects: (i) how climate affects the firm, (ii) how the firm affects the climate.
    Climate risk (outside → inside)
    • Physical: extreme weather, supply disruption, asset damage.
    • Transition: policy, technology, demand shifts, litigation.
    • Financial mapping: cash flow, cost of capital, asset values.
    Typical tools
    • Scenario analysis; stress tests.
    • Geographic exposure mapping.
    • Input price sensitivity (energy/carbon price).
    Environmental performance (inside → outside)
    • Emissions footprint (Scopes 1–2–3).
    • Resource use (energy, water, waste).
    • Product lifecycle impacts.
    Why people mix them up
    A firm can have low emissions but high climate risk (e.g., coastal assets). Or the reverse.
    → Reporting regimes increasingly require both.
    Key distinction
    Risk is about exposure; performance is about impact.

    Carbon accounting as a parallel accounting system

    Carbon accounting borrows the logic of financial reporting: recognition, consolidation, allocation, and assurance.
    Financial accounting analogies
    • Recognition: which activities ‘count’?
    • Consolidation: group boundary (subsidiaries, joint ventures).
    • Allocation: which scope, which product line?
    • Audit: evidence trail and verification.
    Why this matters
    • Managers respond to metrics.
    • Investors rely on comparability.
    • Assurance requires documentation.
    Carbon-specific twists
    • Numbers are often estimated, not measured.
    • Scope 3 forces a value-chain boundary.
    • Double counting is structurally possible.
    Discussion question
    If we treat carbon like accounting: what is the equivalent of earnings management?
    Accounting lens
    Carbon disclosures are governance technologies, not mere engineering.

    The GHG Protocol: principles before numbers

    The Protocol defines principles that mirror accounting norms: relevance, completeness, consistency, transparency, accuracy.
    Five principles (interpretive spine)
    1. Relevance — decision usefulness.
    2. Completeness — disclose exclusions.
    3. Consistency — comparable over time.
    4. Transparency — audit trail; disclose assumptions.
    5. Accuracy — avoid systematic bias; reduce uncertainty.
    A core tension
    Completeness vs accuracy: excluding hard-to-measure sources improves precision but worsens coverage.
    • Protocol warns that a “materiality threshold” can become a predefined negative bias.
    Implication for disclosure
    Good reporting = numbers + boundaries + methods + uncertainty narrative.
    Protocol logic
    Use principles to critique corporate reports.

    What does ‘measuring carbon’ mean?

    GHG inventories translate diverse gases and activities into a single unit: CO₂-equivalent (CO₂e).
    What is counted?
    • CO₂, CH₄, N₂O, and fluorinated gases.
    • Converted via global warming potential into CO₂e.
    Carbon footprint
    • Total GHG emissions associated with an organization, product, or activity.
    • Useful for tracking over time and across units.
    Key idea
    A footprint is not a ‘truth’ — it is a model built from activity data, factors, and boundary rules.
    Discussion question
    Where can bias enter? (activity data, factors, boundaries, exclusions, offsets)
    Unit
    CO₂e enables aggregation—at the cost of modelling assumptions.

    Scope 1–2–3: the architecture of responsibility

    Scopes classify emissions by relationship to the firm: direct operations, purchased energy, and value-chain impacts.
    The intuition
    Scope 1Direct: owned/controlled sources
    Scope 2Indirect: purchased electricity/heat/steam/cooling
    Scope 3All other indirect: upstream + downstream
    Why scopes matter
    • They shape managerial accountability.
    • They drive what firms disclose and manage.
    Managerial implication
    • Scope 1/2 → operational levers (efficiency, fuel switching, renewables).
    • Scope 3 → governance levers (supplier engagement, product design, customer use).
    Discussion question
    If Scope 3 dominates, does ‘the firm’ still mean its legal boundary?
    Core framework
    Scopes are classification rules—not natural categories.

    Boundaries: organizational vs operational

    Inventory design requires two boundary decisions: which entities are in the firm, and which activities count within scopes.
    Organizational boundary (who is ‘in’?)
    • Equity share: emissions proportional to ownership.
    • Control approach: 100% of emissions where the firm has operational/financial control.
    Economic substance can override legal form.
    Operational boundary (what is ‘counted’?)
    • Identify emission sources and classify into Scope 1/2/3.
    • Outsourcing/leasing can move emissions across scopes.
    Boundary choices can change reported performance without changing reality.
    Boundary design
    A central source of incomparability across firms.

    Scope 2: location-based vs market-based

    Scope 2 can be accounted for using grid-average intensity or contractual instruments (RECs, PPAs).
    Two approaches
    • Location-based: reflects average grid emissions where consumption occurs.
    • Market-based: reflects contractual purchases (renewable certificates, PPAs).
    Why it matters
    Market-based accounting can change reported emissions without changing physical electricity flows.
    Benefits
    • Creates incentives to procure renewables.
    • Enables internal targets and planning.
    Critiques
    • Risk of “paper decarbonization”.
    • Additionality questions (would renewables exist anyway?).
    Scope 2 nuance
    Contractual claim vs physical reality.

    Scope 3: why it dominates and why it’s hard

    For many firms, Scope 3 is 70–90%+ of total emissions—yet it is estimated, distributed, and contested.
    Upstream + downstream
    • Upstream: purchased goods/services, capital goods, transport, business travel.
    • Downstream: product use, end-of-life, distribution, franchises.
    Three constraints
    • Low direct control.
    • Supplier data gaps.
    • Use of proxies and averages.
    Governance challenge
    Scope 3 turns measurement into a supply-chain governance project.
    • Supplier engagement and disclosure requirements.
    • Product redesign and demand shaping.
    Discussion question
    Should firms be accountable for emissions they cannot fully control?
    Scope 3
    The most important—and the most disputed—part of the footprint.

    The calculation workflow: activity × factor → CO₂e

    Carbon inventories are largely engineering estimates built from operational data and emission factors—then propagated with uncertainty.
    Core equation
    Activity data × Emission factor = Emissions (CO₂e)
    Example: kWh × kgCO₂/kWh; litres diesel × kgCO₂/litre.
    Where uncertainty comes from
    • Measurement error in activity data.
    • Factor choice (country/technology/time).
    • Boundary choices.
    Quality management (practical)
    1. Define sources (scope map).
    2. Collect activity data (meters, bills, procurement).
    3. Select factors (document source/version).
    4. Compute and QA (reconciliations).
    5. Disclose assumptions + uncertainty.
    Interpretation
    Carbon accounting is not direct measurement but systematic estimation. Small choices in factors, data, and boundaries can materially change reported emissions.
    Carbon accounting mechanics
    Comparable to accounting estimates: assumptions, methods, and audit trail matter.

    Emission factors: the hidden policy choice

    Factor selection embeds assumptions about geography, technology, time, and allocation; it can drive large differences in totals.
    Types of factors
    • Average vs marginal grid factors.
    • National vs supplier-specific factors.
    • Spend-based vs process/LCA-based for Scope 3.
    Tiering intuition
    Moving from generic averages to supplier-specific data increases relevance—often at higher cost.
    Why this matters for incentives
    • Firms may prefer factors that make performance look better.
    • But assurance and stakeholder scrutiny reward transparent, defensible choices.
    Discussion Question
    If two firms use different factors, is the comparison meaningful?
    Estimation choice
    Factors are where ‘science’ meets governance.

    Metrics: absolute, intensity, and the growth problem

    Absolute emissions track real climate impact; intensity metrics aid benchmarking—but can fall while absolute emissions rise.
    Absolute metrics
    • Total tCO₂e (real impact).
    • Required for net-zero claims.
    Strength: climate relevance
    Intensity metrics
    • tCO₂e per revenue / unit / km / user.
    • Useful for growth firms and cross-sectional comparison.
    Risk: ‘decoupling story’ while total rises
    Targets and pathways
    • Base year selection and restatements.
    • Short-term targets vs long-term net zero.
    • Science-based alignment as an external discipline.
    Discussion question
    Which metric would you use for (i) airline (ii) software firm (iii) steel producer—and why?
    Performance metrics
    Metric choice is a strategic communication choice.

    From accounting to management: internal carbon pricing

    When emissions become an internal cost, managers incorporate climate impacts into budgeting, investment, and procurement decisions.
    Internal carbon fee (logic)
    • Charge business units by tCO₂e.
    • Create a central decarbonization fund.
    • Make carbon visible in P&L decisions.
    Manager response channels
    • Improve data quality (to avoid overcharging).
    • Substitute inputs (renewables, efficiency).
    • Engage suppliers (Scope 3 accountability).
    Why it can work
    It aligns micro-incentives with macro targets without waiting for regulation.
    Failure modes
    • Gaming the boundary.
    • Underinvestment if price too low.
    • Conflicts between cost and credibility.
    Strategic tool
    Good design requires measurement discipline + governance.

    Supply chain carbon governance: Apple-style logic

    If Scope 3 is dominant, reductions require supply chain data, standards, and incentives—not just internal efficiency.
    Why Apple’s footprint is mostly Scope 3
    • Contract manufacturing.
    • Materials and components.
    • Product use-phase electricity.
    Governance levers
    • Supplier reporting requirements.
    • Renewable energy procurement standards.
    • Design choices: materials, repairability, energy efficiency.
    A conceptual question
    Is Apple ‘responsible’ for supplier emissions—or merely ‘associated’ with them?
    Debate
    If suppliers serve many brands, who gets the credit for reductions?
    Scope 3 management
    Measurement becomes inter-organizational governance.

    Case: Tesla and the ‘avoided emissions’ debate

    Tesla’s operational footprint can look small relative to its product narrative; the debate is about accounting boundaries and claims.
    Tesla’s reporting frame
    • Emphasize low operational emissions.
    • Highlight ‘emissions avoided’ by EV adoption.
    Why this is contested
    • Avoided emissions are not standard inventory items.
    • Depends on counterfactual (what car is replaced? grid mix?).
    • Risks double counting across actors.
    Critical reading checklist
    1. What boundary is used?
    2. What metric is highlighted?
    3. What assumptions drive the counterfactual?
    4. What is assured vs unaudited?
    Discussion question
    Should avoided emissions be disclosed? If yes, where and how?
    Case study
    ‘measurement claims’ vs ‘inventory numbers’.

    Data quality and uncertainty: the credibility problem

    A GHG inventory can be decision-useful without being perfectly precise—but uncertainty must be managed and disclosed.
    Sources of uncertainty
    • Missing activity data; estimation.
    • Supplier proxies (spend-based).
    • Temporal mismatch (old factors).
    • Boundary changes over time.
    Quality practices
    • Documentation and audit trail.
    • Reconciliations to bills and procurement.
    • Sampling for suppliers; escalation plan.
    Protocol tension
    Completeness vs accuracy is a practical trade-off; credibility comes from transparency.
    Discussion question
    How accurate must data be before management should act?
    Assurance lens
    Credibility comes from transparent assumptions and documented methods

    Strategic measurement and greenwashing risk

    When performance is rewarded, measurement becomes strategic: firms can shift boundaries, metrics, and narratives to look better.
    Common tactics
    • Emphasize intensity over absolute.
    • Highlight market-based Scope 2 only.
    • Selective Scope 3 categories.
    • Offsets substituting for reductions.
    Red flags in reports
    • Big claims with thin methods section.
    • No base-year restatement policy.
    • Unclear scope map; inconsistent time series.
    A governance view
    Greenwashing is often a predictable outcome of measurement + incentives + weak verification.
    Exercise
    Pick a corporate report: identify (i) boundary choices (ii) metric choices (iii) unverifiable claims.
    Critical skills
    Be comfortable challenging ‘nice-looking’ numbers.

    Where carbon accounting is going

    Future improvements likely combine standardization, stronger assurance, better data infrastructure, and more direct measurement technologies.
    Likely directions
    • Interoperability: ISSB/ESRS alignment; digital tagging.
    • Better Scope 3 data: supplier platforms, product passports.
    • MRV tech: sensors, satellite methane detection.
    • Assurance: stronger verification norms and materiality doctrine.
    Design principles for improvement
    1. Make boundaries explicit and comparable.
    2. Separate inventory from impact claims (e.g., avoided emissions).
    3. Disclose uncertainty ranges, not just point estimates.
    4. Reward real reductions over accounting artefacts.
